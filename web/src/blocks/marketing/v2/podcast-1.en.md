<!-- lesson: marketing.podcast-own | Your own podcast: video and audio, where to publish, and how to keep going | 22 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
A company launched a podcast with a big announcement, recorded three episodes with the founder and acquaintances, and stopped at the fourth: there were no guests, editing consumed weekends, and plays numbered fewer than a hundred. A year later, another company started differently: it recorded two episodes in reserve before launch, invited only engineers who had written interesting technical analyses, published every episode on YouTube both in full and as short excerpts, and after one season the podcast had become a source of both future customers and future employees. This lesson covers why an infrastructure company needs its own podcast, where to publish it in 2026, what to record it with, and how to get through the first seven episodes, before which more than half of podcasts stop.

<!-- section: theory | Why an infrastructure company needs a podcast -->
A podcast gives a company conversations with people it wants to know. A project maintainer, an engineer at a large user organization, or the creator of an unusual solution agrees to appear because they can talk about their work; afterward, the company has a relationship with that person and their colleagues. Listeners hear the voices of the company's engineers and learn how they think. An episode becomes several pieces of material: a full video, audio for podcast apps, short excerpts, a transcript, and a blog post. A podcast brings sales slowly, and measuring its success by inquiries in the first month is pointless; its value comes from relationships, recognition, hiring, and material for search.

<!-- section: theory | YouTube and audio: where people listen in 2026 -->
In February 2025, YouTube reported that more than a billion people watched podcasts on it each month, and in October 2025 people watched around 700 million hours of podcasts a month on televisions, versus 400 million a year earlier. In an Edison Research survey from late 2024, 33% of American listeners named YouTube their main podcast platform, 26% named Spotify, and 14% named Apple Podcasts. Meanwhile, a Cumulus and Signal Hill study in fall 2025 found that 92% of podcast listeners had listened during the previous week and only 8% had watched without listening; among those who had recently started a new podcast, 45% discovered it on YouTube. Hosting provider Buzzsprout saw a different picture in October 2026: Apple Podcasts accounted for 30% of downloads of its podcasts and Spotify for 25%. These are different measurements: a survey asks people, while a host counts file downloads, and views on YouTube itself do not appear in that statistic.

The practical choice for an infrastructure podcast follows: publish the full video episode on YouTube and an audio version through an RSS feed for podcast apps. The audio version must make sense without visuals: if a guest shows a terminal or diagram, the host describes what is on screen.

<!-- section: theory | Platform rules -->
A podcast on YouTube is a playlist in which each episode is a separate video. Full episodes should be in the podcast playlist, with promotional excerpts separate; short videos do not appear in YouTube Music. YouTube can import an audio RSS feed and turn each episode into a video with a cover image, but it does not distribute the podcast to other platforms or update an already published video when the audio file in the feed is replaced. The rules do not allow uploading episodes containing inserted advertising from third-party networks, and host-read sponsorships must be labeled as paid promotion.

Since January 2025, Spotify has paid video podcast creators through the Spotify Partner Program; in January 2026, its entry threshold was lowered to three published episodes, 2,000 listening hours, and 1,000 listeners over 30 days, and in September 2026 the program expanded to more than 35 countries. In February 2026, Apple announced a new HLS-based video podcast format available through participating hosting providers; Apple had already supported ordinary video in RSS feeds. Apple generates episode transcripts itself, letting listeners search and jump to the relevant point.

<!-- section: theory | What to record with and where to host -->
The minimum podcast setup includes three things: RSS hosting, remote recording software, and editing software. Hosting costs around $15–25 per month: as of November 2026, Buzzsprout charged $15 for audio and $25 for audio with video when billed annually, Transistor started at $19, and RSS.com offered a free audio plan. Remote recording is done in Riverside, Zencastr, StreamYard, or Descript; the main requirement is recording each participant locally on a separate track so a connection failure does not ruin the episode. Besides recording in Riverside, The Changelog asks guests to make a local backup recording with QuickTime or OBS, wear headphones, sit in a quiet room, and check the lighting. Editing is often done in Descript, where recordings are edited like text; for a technical podcast, watch the software carefully so automatic removal of pauses and filler words does not distort an explanation of a command or flag.

Before the first real episode, record a twenty-minute test with two people, screen sharing, and a deliberately interrupted connection, and check whether the separate tracks recover. Recording service SquadCast stopped being developed in 2026 as users were migrated to a new product, and Descript had a connection outage in April 2026, so a backup recording is always necessary.

<!-- section: theory | Formats and guests -->
Infrastructure podcasts that last for years use several recurring formats. By November 2026, Kubernetes Podcast from Google had released 273 episodes and appeared every two weeks: ecosystem news and a conversation with a maintainer or engineer. Day Two DevOps had accumulated 2.2 million downloads since 2019, and in 2025 its most popular episodes over 90 days were discussions of Kubernetes deployment with kro (3,349 downloads), public and private clouds (3,035), and solving big problems through small ones (2,955). KubeFM invites authors of technical articles and case studies its editors found for their roundups and rarely takes guests without such an article. Rawkode Academy produces long live tool demonstrations.

For a company podcast, this suggests four formats: a maintainer explains one architectural decision; an engineer operating a system reconstructs a real outage; two hosts discuss news using a working example; or a live session fixes a problem in a practice environment. Look for guests through published outage analyses, migration articles, and design documents, and invite the engineer who made the decision; the founder is not always the right person. In the invitation, name the engineering question and how long recording will take. Maintain a guest spreadsheet: topic, article, employer, time zone, invitation status, recording date, permission to publish excerpts, and deadline for corrections.

<!-- section: theory | How to avoid stopping after the third episode -->
An October 2026 Slice study of the Podcast Index catalog counted 4.4 million podcasts, of which 9.4% had released an episode in the previous 90 days. Among podcasts that had stopped, a quarter stopped after the first episode and more than half before the seventh. The research was conducted by a company selling clipping tools, and some apparent stops may have been completed seasons, but the scale is clear.

Podcasts stop when they lack people and guests. In early 2025, The Changelog closed four of its podcasts, including Ship It, and concentrated on its main show and video because several weekly releases left no time for experiments. In his 2025 review, the Day Two DevOps host said that his other podcast had gone on indefinite hiatus and that the company had hired two staff members to edit Day Two DevOps. Start with a season: six episodes released every two weeks, two recorded before launch, and a backup episode without a guest in case of cancellation. After the season, count how many hours each episode took and how listeners responded, and only then decide on another.

<!-- section: theory | What to measure -->
Hosting provider Buzzsprout publishes the distribution of downloads in an episode's first seven days: in October 2026, half of its episodes received up to 27 downloads, an episode with 99 downloads reached the top quarter, and one with 425 reached the top 10%. These are file downloads on one host, with fewer people behind them, but for a niche infrastructure podcast the figures help set realistic expectations for early episodes.

For each episode, record the topic, guest, work hours, downloads after 7, 30, and 90 days, YouTube impressions, clicks and watch time, returning viewers, website visits, practice environment launches, and answers to “How did you hear about us?” Do not combine downloads, Shorts views, and newsletter subscribers into one “reach” figure: they are different people and different actions. Publish an episode page on your own site with a transcript, summary, chapters, software versions, and code links: search engines and AI systems can find such a page, though this does not guarantee citations.

<!-- section: task | Task: a first-season plan -->
Complete this task in a spreadsheet. Choose one format from the lesson and write a podcast name that says what it is about. Find six guests through their published technical work and record the question you are inviting each to discuss. Choose a host, recording software, and editing software, record their prices, and check whether the recording software supports separate tracks for each participant. Schedule recordings of the first two episodes before launch and assign the person who will edit them.
