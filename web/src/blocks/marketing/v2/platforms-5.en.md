<!-- section: hook -->
A founder sent a press release about a new product version to ten industry publications and received no replies. Six months later, an engineer at the same company wrote an article for InfoQ about how the team migrated a customer's databases from virtual machines to Kubernetes and what went wrong. The article was published, cited in two newsletters, and a podcast producer invited the engineer onto the show. This lesson covers how to get into industry media and podcasts when a company has neither a PR agency nor a well-known name.

<!-- section: theory | Articles by company engineers -->
Industry publications for infrastructure audiences, such as InfoQ, Container Journal, and DevOps.com, publish not only their own journalists but also articles submitted by specialists working at companies. Rules change: on July 17, 2026, The New Stack paused its free guest article program and now offers companies a paid placement package, so check a publication's contributor page before submitting an article. Their editors select material for its usefulness to readers and reject pieces that read like advertising. An article that gets accepted usually describes a problem familiar to many readers, a way to solve it, and the cost of that approach; it may mention the company's product, but it should also be useful to someone who chooses a different product.

The CNCF blog works similarly and describes its rules in a public document. Member companies can submit several articles a year depending on their membership level: two for Silver, four for Gold and Platinum. An article must be recent, published no more than two months earlier, vendor-neutral, free of product advertising and attacks on other projects; one external link is allowed in a short conclusion. A response takes seven to ten business days, and publication takes two to three weeks. The course “Open source: project, community, and governance” covers the foundation's opportunities in more detail.

<!-- section: theory | Appearing as a podcast guest -->
Podcasts about Kubernetes and infrastructure are constantly looking for guests who can share something new: project maintainers, engineers operating large systems, and creators of unusual solutions. Well-known English-language podcasts in this area include Kubernetes Podcast from Google, Software Engineering Daily, The Changelog, KubeFM, and Day Two DevOps. A guest pitch works when it contains a topic that interests the podcast's listeners and a brief explanation of why the guest can discuss it better than others. The producer is not interested in your product for its own sake; they are interested in the story.

After an episode is released, its recording becomes material you can use: put the link in documentation, in the newsletter, and on the guest's LinkedIn profile, and cut excerpts into short videos if the podcast permits it. The episode's transcript enters search results and AI training data, so one good appearance brings listeners and readers for years.

<!-- section: theory | Why the press release did not work -->
A press release about a new version interests a journalist only when there is news for their readers behind it: a major customer, a measurable result, a market event, a conflict, or figures nobody else has. A routine product release does not become news for a publication, and journalists receive dozens of these emails a day. The course “Strategy, ecosystem, and PR” covers working with journalists when a company has real news.

<!-- section: task | Task: three article topics and one podcast pitch -->
Complete this task in a document. List three technical stories from your team that would be useful to engineers at other companies, even if they never use your product. For each, find a publication that runs similar material and read its contributor guidelines. Then choose one podcast your buyers listen to, listen to its two latest episodes, and write the producer a five-to-six-sentence email: who the guest is, what they will discuss, and why it interests this podcast's listeners.

<!-- lesson: marketing.platforms-ru | Habr, Telegram, and Russian-language conferences | 16 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
This lesson is for people promoting infrastructure products to Russian-speaking engineers: in Russia, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Belarus, Armenia, Serbia, and among emigrants. This audience has its own platforms, which differ considerably from Western ones: people read technical articles on Habr, news and discussions take place in Telegram channels and chats, and the year's main events are Russian-language conferences with their own program committees. This lesson covers these platforms' rules and prices in 2025–2026.

<!-- section: theory | Habr -->
Habr remains the largest Russian-language platform for technical articles. Companies publish on Habr through a corporate blog with a paid subscription. According to the official 2026 price list for residents, the “Profi” plan costs 220,000 rubles for three months and 660,000 rubles for a year excluding VAT; “Giant” costs 300,000 rubles for three months and 1,000,000 for a year; and “Giant+” costs 350,000 and 1,200,000 rubles. Plans include article analytics, scheduled publishing, Yandex Metrica integration, and, for new customers, a review of their first article. The subscription pays for a place to publish; the company writes the articles itself or commissions them separately.

In June 2026, Habr updated its rules. The platform considers material written primarily to generate sales, collect contacts, or build an audience to be advertising; such material is allowed in corporate blogs and once in the special “I'm promoting myself” section. If a user regularly writes about one company or product, Habr may ask them to start a corporate blog or restrict them. Useful links relevant to the topic are allowed without referral parameters, tracking tags, or shorteners. A product being free or open-source does not exempt it from these rules.

Companies' own reports show what a corporate blog can deliver. By the end of 2025, hosting provider RUVDS had published 753 articles on Habr, with more than 8.3 million views and nearly 40 thousand comments, written by at least 48 authors; the year's most-read article discussed a hidden microphone in a Chinese remote-access device and received more than 212 thousand views. The company did not disclose leads or sales, and interest in an intriguing technical story does not imply interest in buying. More than 51 thousand posts appeared on Habr in 2025, and 84% received a positive rating, so a positive rating alone says little about an article's success.

For an infrastructure company on Habr, it is useful to maintain two streams of articles and compare their results: broad technical stories that introduce the company to people far from buying, and focused analyses of buyers' problems, such as a hosting provider's transition from virtualization to managed services. Since November 2025, Habr has shown a “Reach” metric, which cannot be compared with views on older articles.

<!-- section: theory | Telegram -->
On Telegram, Russian-speaking engineers read channels and talk in chats. A channel is a feed of posts by one author or editorial team, while a chat is a shared conversation where members discuss community topics. Companies pay channels to publish posts, a practice known as seeding. Prices appear in publishers' rate cards: in April 2025, a post in “DevOps Library,” which claimed an average reach of around 1,900 views, cost 17,000 rubles, or around 8,700 rubles per thousand views; in November 2026, the same channel was listed in Tproger's rate card at prices starting from 10,000 rubles, with an audience of around 8 thousand subscribers. In its 2025 results, agency iTrend reported an average seeding cost per thousand views across 112 IT channels of around 16,200 rubles and a cost per subscriber of around 825 rubles in the first half of the year, but these figures mix seeding with official Telegram advertising and describe one agency's campaigns. Nobody has publicly measured how many open-source product installations and paying customers seeding brings in, so start with a small experiment using a separate link for each channel.

Community chats, such as those of DevOpsConf and HighLoad++, are not advertising spaces. It is useful to answer technical questions there and invite people to walkthroughs after agreeing this with moderators. A company's own channel publishing engineering material, community meeting recordings, and announcements works like a newsletter: it is read by people who are already interested in the company.

<!-- section: theory | Conferences -->
The main Russian-language conferences for infrastructure audiences are DevOpsConf, HighLoad++, and Saint HighLoad++, along with conferences and meetups run by individual communities. Major conferences have program committees, open calls for talks, and Telegram channels where they publish deadlines. Organizers rarely publish acceptance rates, and there is no reliable figure for these conferences. Some conferences offer open-source projects a separate way in: tracks or booths for open-source projects. Submission deadlines and partnership terms change every year, so check the conference website and channel. The course “Events and public speaking” covers preparing a proposal and a talk.

<!-- section: task | Task: a seeding experiment and a plan for two article streams -->
Complete this task in a spreadsheet. Choose two Telegram channels whose readers match your buyer profile and record their price, stated reach, and cost per thousand views. Create a separate tagged link for each channel to distinguish visits, and record what result after a month you will consider a success: visits, installations, or messages in the project's chat. Then list two Habr article topics for each of the two streams: broad technical stories and analyses of buyers' problems.
