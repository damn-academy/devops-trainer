<!-- section: hook -->
The startup finance-tool market changed considerably in two years. Bench shut down in December 2024; Mosaic joined HiBob in 2025; Causal joined Lucanet in 2024; Capital One completed its Brex acquisition in April 2026; Stripe completed its Metronome acquisition in January; and Xero raised prices in October. Choosing tools from year-old comparison tables is risky. This lesson groups tools by task with November 2026 prices, shows minimum stacks for ten and forty people, and explains how to test a tool before buying.

<!-- section: theory | Bookkeeping -->
A company should have one general ledger. QuickBooks Online costs from $38 monthly for Simple Start to $340 for Advanced; Plus is $140, and outside accountants are accustomed to it. Since October 2026, Xero costs $27, $59 and $97, with multicurrency only on the highest tier. NetSuite uses custom quotes for annual licensing and implementation and suits multiple entities, consolidation and complex revenue. Puzzle starts at $30 monthly and claims up to 98% automatic categorization: a vendor claim without independent verification. Digits starts at $65. Pilot provides bookkeeping as a service: its entry tier starts at $99 monthly but is cash-basis only; accrual reporting needs a higher, custom-priced tier. After the former Bench shut down, another company continued the service without assuming its obligations, so do not choose based on the old pricing card.

<!-- section: theory | Banking, spending and payments -->
Mercury provides basic banking free, with higher tiers from $29.90; verify partner-bank cash placement against the bank list. After Capital One’s acquisition, Brex offers a free basic tier and Premium at $12 per employee; prepaid funds and bank deposits have different protection. Ramp’s basic plan is free; Plus costs $15 per employee plus a platform fee. Wise Business charges $31 to open and from 0.23% for conversion and transfers; a Wise balance is not an insured deposit.

<!-- section: theory | Billing, usage and subscription metrics -->
Stripe Billing costs 0.7% of billing volume or a subscription from $620 monthly; payment processing, tax and revenue recognition are separate products. Chargebee offers a free base plus 0.8%, or $99 plus 0.65%. Maxio starts at $599 for billings up to $100,000 monthly. For usage: Metronome, now part of Stripe, charges 0.8% plus four cents per thousand events; Orb uses credits, around 0.6% of volume plus event charges. For subscription metrics, ChartMogul is free up to $10,000 MRR, then starts at $69; Baremetrics starts at $75. Their metrics may differ from definitions approved for your board.

At $100,000 monthly billings, Stripe costs $700, Chargebee $749, and Metronome $800 plus $400 for ten million events. This is not an all-in comparison: terms, calculation bases, supported contracts and minimums differ. Total tool cost includes fixed fees, billing percentages, events, seats, add-ons, implementation and your employees’ maintenance time.

<!-- section: theory | Modeling, equity and payroll -->
For a team of 5–15, Google Sheets or Excel following the model lesson is sufficient. Dedicated services cost considerably more: Runway Financial, now cfo.ai, starts at $375 monthly on annual billing; Causal, Mosaic and Abacum pricing is on request following ownership changes. For cap tables, Carta Launch is free for companies with fewer than 25 stakeholders and less than one million raised; Cake starts at $1,000 annually for 25 stakeholders, with 409A valuations in higher tiers; Pulley pricing is on request. US payroll through Gusto starts at $49 plus $6 per employee on the single-state basic tier, or $80 plus $12 on Plus. Rippling starts at $8 per employee plus a base fee. For foreign employees without your own entity, an employer of record is needed: Deel and Remote start at $599 monthly per employee. Comparing that with US payroll pricing is meaningless; they are different services.

<!-- section: theory | Minimum stacks for ten and forty people -->
A ten-person company needs one general ledger (QuickBooks Plus or Xero Growing), one main bank with cards and one independent backup bank, Gusto covering the relevant states, Stripe Billing, revenue and prepaid schedules in a controlled spreadsheet, a monthly bookkeeper and quarterly tax adviser. Calculate software costs using your own inputs: the general-ledger plan, Gusto's base fee plus its per-employee charge, and Stripe Billing's pay-as-you-go share of billing volume, 0.7%, or $700 monthly on $100,000 of invoices. There is no single figure for everyone: at low billing volumes, the stack costs a few hundred dollars monthly, while billing becomes the largest item as revenue grows. Cap table, tax services, bookkeeping and CFO services are excluded from this calculation. The main resource is a person responsible for month-end close; another analytics service cannot replace them.

A forty-person company needs a finance owner, a payables/bookkeeping role, a fractional CFO and tax adviser, separate payment approvers, CRM contracts, billing and usage systems, a revenue register, QuickBooks Advanced or NetSuite for multiple entities, spending and payroll services, a data warehouse for product margins and cohort metrics, and a model using closed accounting data. Contract, customer and department IDs must be consistent across systems.

<!-- section: theory | Testing a tool before buying -->
A vendor demo is insufficient. Ask the vendor to process ten real contracts and cases: annual prepayment, an escalating enterprise contract, usage with minimums and overages, a foreign-currency invoice, a credit note, an employee termination and SAFE conversion. Check refunds, credits and partial payments; full ledger and attachment exports; change logs, roles and period locks; data ownership; annual-renewal cancellation; and the cost of migrating history and leaving. “AI” in the description does not replace this test.

<!-- lesson: finance.automation | Accounting automation: banks, Stripe, spreadsheets and testing “smart” bookkeeping | 22 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
Bookkeeping services promise automatic categorization, and tools such as Zapier and n8n promise to connect Stripe, the bank and a spreadsheet in an evening. Automation saves hours but has a characteristic failure: it multiplies wrong entries as quickly as correct ones. A redelivered webhook doubles revenue, a Stripe payout becomes revenue again, and a closed month quietly changes after investor reporting. This lesson covers worthwhile automation, Stripe reconciliation, reliable spreadsheet workflows and testing “smart” bookkeeping on your own data.

<!-- section: theory | What to automate and how to check it -->
Every automated flow needs its own check. Bank statements into the ledger: completeness against statement totals, without duplicating pending and posted transactions. Vendor categorization rules: new vendors and large variances enter a review queue. Receipt OCR: check currency, duplicate images and business purpose. Stripe into the ledger through an invoice register: invoices, payments, refunds, fees, disputes and payouts are distinct transaction types, reconciled through the Stripe clearing account. Prepaids and equipment: an approved schedule generates monthly entries and stops on the end date. Hosting accruals: estimate usage at approved prices minus amounts already billed, with reversal. Payroll into the ledger: accruals, employer taxes, benefits, liabilities and payments reconcile to register totals. The model draws only from closed periods with export timestamps. Failed-sync, overdue-AR, below-floor cash and duplicate-invoice alerts have owners and deadlines.

<!-- section: theory | A Stripe reconciliation example -->
A customer is invoiced 1,000 plus sales tax of 80, totaling 1,080. Stripe’s fee is 30 and bank payout 1,050. Service is delivered now: AR 1,080, revenue 1,000 and tax payable 80. Customer payment: Stripe clearing 1,080 and AR −1,080. Fee: expense 30 and clearing −30. Payout: bank 1,050 and clearing −1,050. The clearing account ends at zero. For an annual prepayment, the customer liability rises instead of revenue, which follows its schedule. Refunds and disputes are separate transactions linked to the original invoice.

<!-- section: theory | A reliable spreadsheet workflow -->
For Google Apps Script, Zapier or n8n, use one sequence: signed webhook or scheduled API request; immutable raw table; deduplication; mapping of customer, account, currency, tax and period; validation; draft entry or exception; approved entry; reconciliation and export. The idempotency key combines system, object ID, event type and version. Stripe documents repeated, delayed and out-of-order webhook delivery, so verify signatures before processing and skip already processed events.

If an event belongs to a closed period, a customer or account cannot be mapped, or the amount differs materially, create an owned exception instead of an entry. Every entry’s debits and credits must match, and the source-to-entry link must be retained. Protect the raw spreadsheet sheet, calculate metrics using stable IDs, and give triggers retries with backoff, run logs, last-success timestamps and stale-data alerts. Apps Script quotas depend on account type, so do not build payroll-critical processes on it. Never put bank passwords in cells; give connectors minimal permissions.

<!-- section: theory | Testing “smart” bookkeeping -->
Promises such as “up to 98% automatic categorization” or “round-the-clock AI bookkeeping and reconciliation” are vendor claims without published independent validation. Test them on your own data. Take an already closed month, hide the accountant’s classifications, and run 200 typical transactions, every transaction over $5,000 and nonstandard contracts through the service. Measure accuracy by account, department, period and tax; dollar error; transactions posted without a human; reconciliation differences; and hours saved after review. Adopt only when no unexplained material difference remains, every entry traces to its source and rollback has been tested. AI does not receive authority to change bank details or send payments based on invoice text.

<!-- quiz -->
? Stripe paid $1,050 into the bank. What should be recorded?
+ A transfer from Stripe clearing to the bank, with no new revenue || Revenue was recognized when service was delivered; payout moves cash between accounts.
- Revenue of $1,050 || This duplicates revenue already recognized at invoicing.
- Revenue of 1,080 and a fee of 30 to show the customer’s full payment || Revenue and fees were already recorded at payment; payout does not repeat them.
? A Stripe webhook arrived twice. How do you avoid duplicating the entry?
+ Check a key combining system, object ID, event type and version; skip processed events || Redelivery is normal webhook behavior and the workflow must account for it.
- Disable webhook redelivery in Stripe so every event arrives once || Retries prevent lost events during failures.
- Delete duplicates manually at month-end when reconciling the clearing account || Manual cleanup is unreliable and late.
<!-- /quiz -->
