<!-- lesson: finance.taxes-federal | Federal and Delaware taxes: calendar, franchise tax, Section 174 and research credit | 30 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
Every winter, Delaware startup founders receive franchise-tax notices for tens of thousands of dollars and panic. For this lesson’s company with ten million authorized shares, the method Delaware shows first produces $85,165, while the second permitted method produces $1,250. Choosing the method saves almost the entire amount. Federal taxes have similar traps: a company reporting losses can owe income tax, and development-cost rules changed twice in three years. This lesson covers federal and Delaware deadlines, franchise tax, income tax, Section 174 after the 2025 law, and the research credit offset against payroll taxes. This is an educational discussion, not tax advice: make company-specific decisions with a tax adviser.

<!-- section: theory | The obligations calendar -->
For a calendar-year company, the main dates are:

- Delaware annual report and franchise tax for 2026: March 1, 2027.
- Federal corporate income-tax return, Form 1120, for 2026: April 15, 2027. A Form 7004 extension to October 15 extends filing, not payment.
- Federal estimated income-tax payments: April 15, June 15, September 15 and December 15 if expected tax after credits is at least $500.
- Contractor Forms 1099-NEC for 2026 payments: February 1, 2027, because January 31 falls on Sunday.
- Employee Forms W-2 and W-3 for 2026: also February 1, 2027.
- Quarterly payroll-tax return, Form 941: the last day of the month following the quarter; federal unemployment tax, Form 940: annually, with the 2026 return due February 1, 2027. Payroll taxes themselves are deposited earlier according to a schedule: if Form 941 payroll taxes in the lookback period—the four quarters from July 1 of the year before last through June 30 of last year—did not exceed $50,000, the company pays monthly, by the 15th of the following month; above that threshold, it pays semiweekly, with the deadline depending on payday: taxes on wages paid Wednesday through Friday are deposited by the following Wednesday, and those on wages paid Saturday through Tuesday by the following Friday. A new company usually pays monthly in its first year. Federal unemployment tax is deposited separately, quarterly by the last day of the month following the quarter, if the accumulated amount exceeds $500. If a deadline falls on a weekend or federal holiday, it moves to the next business day. An exception applies to everyone, including new companies: accumulate payroll tax within one deposit period—a calendar month for monthly depositors, or separately for wages paid Wednesday through Friday and Saturday through Tuesday for semiweekly depositors. Once the total within that period reaches $100,000, deposit it on the next business day. For example, $60,000 accrued on Thursday and $40,000 on Friday fall in the same Wednesday–Friday period; the threshold is reached on Friday and the money is deposited on Monday. A monthly depositor reaching the threshold switches to the semiweekly schedule from the following day through the end of the next calendar year. The company determines its own annual schedule before January 1 from taxes in the lookback period; make this the first item in the annual calendar. If an IRS schedule notice arrives, compare it with that calculation. If accumulated federal unemployment tax at year-end is $500 or less, pay it with Form 940 or by its due date. The semiweekly schedule has a further qualification: depositors have at least three business days after the end of the pay period; Saturdays, Sundays and official District of Columbia holidays do not count as business days for this rule, so a holiday moves the deadline to the next business day. The $100,000 next-day rule takes precedence: it applies even if the ordinary schedule would still allow three business days.
- Founder 83(b) election: within thirty days of restricted-stock transfer.
- Beneficial ownership information (BOI): US-created entities have been exempt since August 2026 under FinCEN’s final rule.
- State obligations follow their own rules, covered in the next lesson.

Assign an internal calendar owner even if Gusto runs payroll and an accountant prepares returns. Record jurisdiction, obligation, period, filing and payment deadlines, extension, owner, preparer, accrued and paid amounts, confirmation number and notice status.

<!-- section: theory | Delaware franchise tax -->
Delaware offers two corporate franchise-tax methods, and the company pays the lower amount. The authorized-shares method charges $175 for up to 5,000 shares, $250 up to 10,000, and another $85 for every additional 10,000 shares or fraction thereof. The assumed par value capital method has a $400 minimum; capital is rounded up to millions, at $400 per million. The ordinary maximum is $200,000, or $250,000 for large corporate filers. A separate $50 annual report fee applies.

The example company has ten million authorized shares, eight million issued shares, par value of $0.00001 and gross assets of two million. Authorized-shares tax: 250 + 999 × 85 = $85,165. Under assumed capital, assumed par equals assets divided by issued shares: two million / eight million = $0.25. Assumed capital is 0.25 × ten million = 2.5 million; tax is 3 × 400 = 1,200, or 1,250 including the fee. Delaware notices often show the authorized-shares calculation; you need not pay it, because the second method is permitted and selected when filing. This formula is simplified for one low-par-value share class; use the official calculator for multiple classes or no-par shares. Gross assets are neither net assets nor the investor valuation.

If tax is $5,000 or more, pay instalments: 40% by June 1, 20% each by September 1 and December 1, and the remainder by March 1. Late payment brings a $200 penalty and 1.5% monthly interest.

<!-- section: theory | Income tax: a book loss does not mean zero tax -->
The federal corporate income-tax rate is 21% of taxable income; neither revenue nor bank cash is the tax base. A corporation generally files Form 1120 even without profit. State taxes are calculated separately.

Taxable income can differ greatly from book income. Example: US GAAP pretax loss is −200; foreign development costs of 300 are expensed immediately in the books but amortized for tax, with only 10 deductible in year one, adding back 290; another 10 is nondeductible expense. Taxable income is 100 and federal tax 21 despite a book loss. Net operating loss carryforwards and tax credits may reduce the tax, but ownership changes under Section 382 can limit the use of prior losses.

<!-- section: theory | Section 174 and the 2025 law -->
From 2022 through 2024, US companies had to capitalize research and development costs, including software development, and amortize US work over five years and foreign work over fifteen, using a midyear convention. For startups, this could mean tax despite losses. In material published by a House committee in 2024, the owner of small company Dynamoid reported about $100,000 in tax despite a loss of roughly $30,000. Her company was an LLC taxed as an S corporation, so the amount cannot be transferred to a corporation, but the mechanism is the same.

The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act introduced Section 174A from tax year 2025, again allowing immediate deductions for US research and development, including software. Foreign development remains amortized over fifteen years. Classification depends on where work actually happens, not where the contractor is incorporated. Example: one million of US development costs. Under the old rules, year-one deduction was 100; under the new rules, the entire million. With enough taxable income, the first-year federal tax difference is 900 × 21% = 189. This is timing: the company receives no government cash, and it is not a tax credit.

For unamortized 2022–2024 costs, the law provided transition options: deduct the balance in the first year after 2024 or equally over two years. Small companies with revenue up to $31 million could apply the new rules retroactively. The retroactive-election deadline expired July 6, 2026; in November you cannot write “file by July.” Check what was elected on time. In September 2026, the IRS issued Rev. Proc. 2026-32, changing accounting-method procedures without extending that expired deadline. Separately check whether your state conforms to federal rules: many do not automatically.

Maintain a development-cost register: project, technical uncertainty, work, employee or contractor, work location, hours, cost allocation, domestic or foreign, tax year, elected method and return evidence.

<!-- section: theory | Research credits and payroll-tax offsets -->
The Section 41 research credit reduces tax; it is not a deduction. A small company may offset payroll taxes even without profit if current-year gross receipts are below $5 million and it had no gross receipts earlier than five years before the current year; receipts of related companies are counted together. Check the Form 6765 instructions for the precise conditions. The maximum is $500,000 annually for at most five years. Calculate and substantiate the credit; employing engineers does not qualify automatically.

Work must have a permitted purpose, be technological, and resolve uncertainty through experimentation. Routine support and foreign research do not qualify. A simplified alternative-method example: current costs of one million and a three-year historical average of 600. The unadjusted credit is 14% × (1,000 − 50% × 600) = 98. Electing the reduced credit at a 21% tax rate gives 98 × 79% = 77.4.

Claim the credit on Form 6765 with the income-tax return, then apply it on Form 8974 with quarterly payroll returns starting in the quarter after filing. It first reduces employer Social Security tax, up to 250 per quarter, then employer Medicare; the remainder carries forward. If a quarter has 20 of eligible Social Security and 5 of Medicare, 25 of the 77.4 is used and 52.4 carries forward. Beginning with tax year 2026, Form 6765 requires Section G project-level information, with small-company exceptions. Keep project evidence and time allocation during development: retrospectively proving “90% of engineering salaries” is difficult.

<!-- lesson: finance.taxes-people | States, contractors, payroll and founder shares | 28 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
A company is incorporated in Delaware, but its first employee lives in California. From that day, it may face California registration, franchise tax starting at $800 annually, state payroll taxes and unemployment insurance. A designer in Portugal needs Form W-8BEN, not a 1099. Founder and employee shares may qualify for QSBS tax benefits, which became more generous in 2025. For shares subject to a repurchase right, a timely 83(b) election largely determines the holding period and tax basis; an unexercised option does not itself provide this benefit. This lesson covers state obligations, SaaS sales tax, US and foreign contractors, payroll taxes, QSBS and 83(b), beneficial ownership reporting and audits.

<!-- section: theory | States: four separate obligations -->
Delaware incorporation does not remove obligations elsewhere, and there are four distinct types. Foreign qualification means registration of an out-of-state company: a Delaware corporation operating in California, not a foreign-country company. State income or franchise tax follows the state’s nexus rules. Sales tax has its own economic-nexus rules. Employer registration covers withholding and unemployment insurance. One remote employee can create several obligations at once.

California, for example, imposes franchise tax on companies doing business there even if they failed to register: a minimum of $800 and a corporate rate of 8.84%, with a first-year minimum exemption for some new companies. Before every hire, complete a register row: state, work and home addresses, start date, qualification, income-tax account, withholding account, unemployment account, workers’ compensation, local taxes, sales-tax nexus, filing frequency and owner. An EOR or PEO does not automatically eliminate corporate nexus questions.

<!-- section: theory | Sales tax on SaaS -->
After the Supreme Court’s 2018 South Dakota v. Wayfair decision, physical presence is no longer the only basis for sales tax. Each state defines economic nexus and whether software and cloud services are taxable. There is no federal SaaS sales tax. Examples as of November 2026:

- California generally exempts electronically delivered software without a physical copy; the economic-nexus threshold is $500,000 in sales.
- New York generally taxes prewritten software, including remote access; the threshold is more than $500,000 and more than 100 sales over four quarters.
- Texas generally taxes SaaS and data processing with a 20% exemption, taxing 80% of the price; its threshold is $500,000 over twelve months.
- Washington taxes remote software access and has a separate B&O tax; its threshold is $100,000, and the 2025 expansion to IT services needs review.

Texas example: a 10,000 invoice for qualifying data processing at an 8.25% rate has taxable base 8,000 and tax 660; the customer pays 10,660. Company revenue is 10,000; 660 is a liability to the state. Maintain a sales table with product, service description, customer usage location, taxable share, nexus status and date, registration, exemption certificate, rate, tax collected and return number. Automated tax services need correct product codes and registrations; they do not decide legal taxability for the founder.

<!-- section: theory | US and foreign contractors -->
Collect Form W-9 from US contractors before first payment and verify name and tax ID. From 2026, Form 1099-NEC is required for annual service payments of at least $2,000; the previous threshold was $600. The threshold applies to US contractors who are individuals or partnerships; payments to corporations generally do not require the form, with exceptions such as legal services, while foreign contractors need W-8BEN instead. Do not duplicate payments reported by card and payment platforms. Without a correct tax ID, 24% backup withholding applies.

Collect W-8BEN from a foreign individual and W-8BEN-E from a foreign company. The payer retains the form; it is not “filed with the IRS.” Record where work actually occurred: service income sourcing follows work location, not a US bank or payment currency. Work performed entirely outside the US generally has no US withholding with proper documentation. US work, royalties and treaty claims change the analysis. W-8 does not prove contractor rather than employee status or replace an IP assignment agreement.

Form 5472 is triggered not by foreign contractors but by a corporate foreign owner holding at least 25% and related-party transactions; the nonfiling penalty is $25,000. A foreign subsidiary may require Form 5471, FBAR reporting and Section 482 transfer-pricing support. An 8% subsidiary cost markup is not a safe harbor; it requires analysis.

<!-- section: theory | Payroll taxes -->
In 2026, employers pay Social Security at 6.2% on wages up to $184,500 per employee and Medicare at 1.45% without a cap. Additional Medicare of 0.9% above $200,000 is withheld only from employees. On salary of $180,000, the employer pays $11,160 Social Security and $2,610 Medicare, totaling $13,770 annually before federal and state unemployment, benefits and workers’ compensation. So “a $180,000 salary costs $180,000” is wrong. State withholding, interstate reciprocity and remote-work rules depend on work and residence locations. Outsourcing payroll does not remove employer responsibility to the IRS. Before paying, review the payroll register, hires and departures and bank cash; afterward, reconcile expenses and liabilities and verify filing confirmations.

<!-- section: theory | QSBS and 83(b) -->
Section 1202 (QSBS) excludes part of the gain on qualifying small-business stock from federal tax. For shares acquired after July 4, 2025, the 2025 law introduced exclusions of 50% after three years, 75% after four and 100% after five. The exclusion cap is $15 million or ten times stock basis; the company’s gross-asset threshold at issuance is $75 million. Conditions include a noncorporate holder, a US C corporation, original-issue stock, an active qualifying business and redemption restrictions. The exclusion is federal; states may not conform. Example: an eight-million gain on new shares after three years excludes four million. Retain issuance and basis documents, balance sheets, the cap table and redemption history.

File an 83(b) election within thirty days of receiving vesting stock; do not wait for vesting or the first tax return. A founder buys vesting shares for $1,000 at a fair market value of $1,000. A timely election makes the current taxable difference zero, and value increases at each vesting do not become compensation income. Without the election, each vesting may create income at the then-current share value. Use Form 15620 or a written election, retain proof of timely mailing and give the company a copy. An ordinary option is not a restricted-share transfer, so 83(b) does not apply to it.

<!-- section: theory | Beneficial ownership reporting and audits -->
FinCEN’s August 2026 final rule exempted US-created entities and their owners from Corporate Transparency Act beneficial ownership reporting. 2024 checklists with a universal filing deadline are outdated. Bank checks and tax disclosures still apply.

“A Series B audit is legally mandatory” is false. A private startup may need an audit under investor or lender agreements, a board decision, buyer requirements or preparations for an IPO. A financial-statement audit is not SOC 2. Six to nine months before the first audit, review customer contracts, opening balances, equity and options, related parties, capitalized costs and tax calculations.
