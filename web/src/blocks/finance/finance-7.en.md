<!-- section: hook -->
A founder who checks the banking app daily can still miss an approaching cash shortfall without seeing the forecast, overdue invoices and rising cloud costs. The opposite mistake is a forty-metric dashboard nobody opens. This lesson brings the course together into a short routine: what to review weekly, monthly and quarterly, what action each threshold triggers, and how to put company finances in order in the first thirty days.

<!-- section: theory | Every week -->
- Available cash: unrestricted account balances accessible immediately. Below the next payroll, taxes and four weeks of mandatory expenses, hold a daily cash meeting.
- The thirteen-week forecast minimum and headroom above the floor. If any week has negative headroom, decide before that week.
- Collections versus forecast. A shortfall over $10,000 or 10% triggers review of the largest invoices and customer calls.
- Overdue AR and its concentration. Every material invoice more than thirty days overdue gets an owner, date and payment plan.
- Cloud cost per unit. Growth above 10% in a week without a mix explanation triggers workload and abuse checks.
- Data freshness. Bank or billing data more than a day old is a failure to fix first.

<!-- section: theory | Every month -->
- MRR and ARR bridge: opening, new, expansion, contraction and churn. Do not publish a final total without customer-level reconciliation.
- NRR and GRR for annual cohorts. GRR below 90%, or NRR below 100% where expansion was expected, triggers a retention review.
- Gross margin by product. A decline of more than five points versus plan triggers review of mix, unit costs and support.
- Gross, net and all-in cash burn. More than 10% worse than forecast for two consecutive months triggers reforecasting.
- Downside runway without unsigned financing. Below twelve months, prepare a financing or spending-reduction plan; below six, an explicit survival plan.
- CAC and payback by segment. More than eighteen months or deterioration across three consecutive cohorts triggers channel-spending review.
- Plan versus actuals. Budget owners explain every variance exceeding both 5% and $5,000.
- Close health. If close takes more than ten days or material balances remain unexplained, the board report waits.
- Deferred revenue and cloud commitments. If forecast cash does not cover delivery and renewals, reallocate liquidity.

<!-- section: theory | Every quarter -->
- Burn multiple. Above 2 for two consecutive quarters, or no ARR growth, triggers review of growth spending.
- ARR per employee, with and without contractors. A decline unexplained by stage or hiring triggers a capacity review.
- Rule of 40 and magic number: trend diagnostics, not seed-stage pass/fail tests.
- Customer concentration: the largest and top-five customers’ shares of revenue, collections and renewal dates. If losing the largest breaches the cash floor, prepare a contingency plan.
- Cash concentration by bank and uninsured share. A breached bank limit or untested backup payment route triggers reallocation and testing.
- Compliance: state register, taxes filed and paid, option grants, 83(b) and 409A. Any missed deadline gets a remediation owner the same day.

For every board-dashboard metric, show actual, prior period, budget, latest forecast, downside, definition version and source update time side by side. A green metric backed by stale data is an interface error. All thresholds here are starting rules for a small company, not industry standards; adapt them to size and stage.

<!-- section: theory | The stories behind these rules -->
These rules have practical roots. Bench in December 2024 showed that access to books must not depend on one supplier. Kubient and Comscore showed that revenue unsupported by delivered services eventually gets corrected, at a cost. Dynamoid showed that book losses do not guarantee zero tax. Rippling and Roku during SVB’s collapse showed that bank cash is not the same as cash available for payroll. Gumroad in 2019 cut three quarters of its team and survived as a smaller profitable business; Buffer in 2016 hired faster than cash grew. None proves a single cause of success or failure, but each shows a check that should have happened earlier.

<!-- section: theory | The first 30 days -->
If company finances are disorganized, you can put them in order within a month.

- Days 1–3: inventory all bank, card, payroll and processor accounts, access and documents; confirm cash with statements; ensure the next payroll is funded.
- Days 4–7: build a thirteen-week forecast using actual dates; assign AR and AP owners; create a tax calendar and state/foreign-ownership questionnaire.
- Week two: chart of accounts, COGS allocation policy, deferred revenue register, vendor approvals and backup export.
- Week three: the first closed month with a balanced balance sheet, matching direct and indirect cash flows, reconciled bank and processor, and plan-versus-actual review.
- Week four: hiring forecast, base and downside scenarios, cap-table reconciliation, dashboard and a short investor update.

<!-- quiz -->
? A dashboard metric is green, but bank data has not updated for three days. What does this mean?
+ An interface error: a metric backed by stale data cannot be trusted || Show source update times alongside every metric.
- Everything is fine because bank cash cannot change much in three days || Payroll or a large refund can occur in three days.
- Recolor the metric yellow and wait for the week-end data update || Data failures should be fixed immediately.
? Downside runway without another round is eight months. What do the dashboard rules call for?
+ Prepare financing or spending cuts because runway is below twelve months || Eight months leaves little time for a round that takes several months.
- Nothing, because investor discussions are underway and the round will likely close on time || Unsigned rounds do not belong in the downside case.
- Immediately announce a survival plan with layoffs because less than a year remains || The survival-plan threshold is below six months.
