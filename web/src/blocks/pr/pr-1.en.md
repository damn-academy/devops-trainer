<!-- lesson: pr.analysts | Analysts: how Gartner and others work | 14 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
An analyst-relations agency offered to “get into the Magic Quadrant” for a founder for 30 thousand dollars. The founder almost signed the contract, but a colleague showed Gartner's official policy: a subscription does not buy inclusion in research, and an analyst briefing is free and does not require being a client. This lesson explains how working with analyst firms works and what a small company can expect from it.

<!-- section: theory | Briefings and research -->
Gartner accepts briefing requests from companies that do not buy its services; analysts agree if the topic is relevant to their research, and payment plays no part. A briefing is on the record, lasts around 45 minutes, involves one or two analysts, and is usually scheduled two to four weeks after the request is accepted. A briefing gives the analyst information; it is not a consultation. In June 2025, Gartner clarified that evaluative research is separate from advisory work: inclusion depends on the criteria of the specific study, and analysts cannot use paid consultations to coach a vendor on improving its position.

Research formats differ. A Magic Quadrant evaluates vendors on Ability to Execute and Completeness of Vision in a specific market. A Hype Cycle places technologies and practices along stages of expectations and may name sample vendors. A Market Guide describes a market without evaluation. Being a sample vendor in a Hype Cycle is not equivalent to being a Leader in a Magic Quadrant, and there is no universal revenue threshold for inclusion in these formats. Grafana announced its Leader position in the Observability Platforms Magic Quadrant on 10 July 2025, and revenue of more than 400 million dollars two and a half months later; this is not a benchmark for a small company.

<!-- section: theory | What to bring to a briefing -->
Bring a working product, the buyer's problem, the deployment model, named competitors, customers willing to speak to the analyst, and evidence that the product can be supported. Ask which research covers your problem, and do not assume the relevant Magic Quadrant exists. An accepted briefing and an analyst's accurate understanding of your product are intermediate results.

Gartner does not allow invented analyst quotes praising a vendor or product. Quotes for a press release must come from published research, usually no more than twelve months old, and require approval from the compliance department; an analyst's spoken remarks cannot be published. Public prices for subscriptions, consultations, and report reprint rights are unavailable, so keep briefings, paid advisory work, research access, reprint licenses, and agency services as separate budget lines.

<!-- section: task | Task: a briefing request -->
Complete this task in a document. Find the research on Gartner's, Forrester's, or IDC's website that is closest to your category, and record its name, format, and author. Prepare a briefing request: the buyer's problem, deployment model, three competitors, two customers willing to speak, and what new information you can offer the analyst.

<!-- lesson: pr.press | PR: when a press release works and how to speak to journalists | 20 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
A company issues a press release for every product release, distributes it through an agency for several hundred dollars, and gets reprints on websites nobody reads. One day, a journalist from The Register contacted a company engineer directly because the engineer had posted a blog analysis of how VMware's new licensing policy changed the cost of a cluster on the company's own hardware, with figures and a table. This lesson covers when a press release is useful, how infrastructure publications work, and how exclusives and embargoes work.

<!-- section: theory | What journalists think -->
In 2025, Cision surveyed more than three thousand journalists across 19 markets: 72 % called press releases the most useful resource PR professionals provide, 86 % reject pitches outside their beat and audience, and 72 % worry about factual errors in AI-written PR materials. Cision sells PR services, and the sample is not limited to infrastructure journalists, but the conclusion is clear: a press release is useful as a source of facts if it contains news for a particular journalist's readers.

News for an infrastructure publication is a measurable result, a market event, figures nobody else has, a conflict, or a decision with consequences. In September 2025, Grafana announced funding alongside revenue and customer figures, and those figures gave journalists context. For a technical release, prepare a working artifact, versions and availability, migration implications, limitations, and an engineer who can answer questions. For funding news, distinguish new money from sales of existing shares, and do not present company valuation as product adoption.

<!-- section: theory | What press release distribution costs -->
As of November 2026, Business Wire starts at 475 dollars for 400 words on a local US wire, with length, geography, multimedia, and translation increasing the price. EIN Presswire sells one release for 149 dollars, a package for 499, and fifteen for 999. PR Newswire does not publish prices. Wire distribution guarantees that the text appears on sites that republish releases, but not an editorial article.

<!-- section: theory | Publications and journalists -->
The Register asks for tips backed by evidence and publishes journalists' beats: as of late 2026, Liam Proven covers Linux and open source, Dan Robinson covers infrastructure, and Tobias Mann covers systems; assignments change, so check the live page. On 17 July 2026, The New Stack paused its independent guest-article program and sells partner placements; paid placement does not count as editorial coverage. InfoWorld suggests contacting authors through their bylines, maintains a practitioner expert program, and separately labels sponsored BrandPost articles. Heise separates editorial and advertising contact forms; a German-language explanation and verifiable local customers are a better fit for a sovereignty or virtualization story in DACH. SDxCentral separates editorial and commercial teams.

An exclusive is an agreement that one publication gets to publish the news first for a defined period. An embargo is an agreement not to publish before a precise time with a time zone, and it applies only if the journalist explicitly accepts it; an unsolicited document marked “under embargo” imposes no obligation on the journalist. A good launch package separates what is already available from promises, includes a customer or maintainer quote, makes commercial terms verifiable, and offers the journalist time with the people who built or operate the product.

<!-- section: theory | Help from a foundation -->
CNCF helps projects with announcements according to their maturity level: on entering Incubating, a project blog post, an embargoed media pitch, and a post on announcement day; for releases, webinars, posts, and press outreach. These are services for projects, not a substitute for a PR agency, and the foundation does not guarantee press coverage. Member companies participate in quarterly new-member announcements. The claim that “the Linux Foundation will issue a free press release for any member” is unsupported. The next lesson covers crisis communication, when the news is bad.

<!-- section: theory | Two pitches to a journalist: analysis -->
Imagine Stratova, a fictional private-cloud platform company. In the past year twelve European hosting providers leaving VMware moved to its platform. One provider in Czechia is willing to discuss its migration on the record. The company has published measurements of cluster costs on its own hardware before and after migration.

The first pitch: “Hello! We are Stratova, the market leader in open cloud platforms. We would like to offer you an exclusive story about our revolutionary platform. We would be happy to give an interview!” The journalist will not read beyond the first line: there is no news, the claims have no evidence, and the relevance to readers is unclear.

The second pitch: “Hello Dan. In October you wrote about European providers recalculating their costs after VMware's new terms. We have data on twelve providers that moved to an open platform this year. One, based in Brno, is willing to discuss the migration costs and what went wrong on the record. Here is a link to cluster cost measurements before and after. If this interests you, our CTO can answer questions this week. If it does not, no reply is needed and I will not follow up.” This pitch connects to the journalist's previous article, offers verifiable facts and an independent speaker, and makes declining easy.

Check your pitch against five questions: does it connect to the journalist's work; does it include a verifiable fact; can someone outside your company confirm the story; do the first two sentences explain the point; and is declining easy? If you have no news of your own, write a pitch using the fictional Stratova facts above.

<!-- section: task | Task: news for one journalist -->
Complete this task in a document. Choose one publication from the lesson and find a journalist who has covered your category in the last three months. Read three of their articles. Then write down which story from your company could be news for their readers: the fact at its core, the figures, who can discuss it, and what the journalist can verify independently. Write a pitch email of six to eight sentences.
