<!-- section: hook -->
A regional bank decided to leave VMware and wanted to evaluate an open private cloud platform built by a small company of thirty people. The bank's head of infrastructure warned right away that the bank could not buy it directly: all of its hardware and software is supplied and supported by one integrator under a framework agreement. Five months later the bank signed, but for less and later than the vendor expected, and along the way the integrator went silent for six weeks, showed the bank a competitor's platform, a second partner tried to undercut the price, and the head of infrastructure offered to bypass the integrator. This lesson walks through that deal step by step: who said what, what the vendor did, where it went wrong, and how partner programs, deal registration and co-selling work at large infrastructure software vendors.

<!-- section: theory | Three parties and the money between them -->
A deal through an integrator has three parties. The vendor builds the software and sells licenses or subscriptions. The integrator, also called a partner or reseller, resells the software to the customer with its own markup and, more importantly, sells the customer work: design, migration, implementation and support. The customer pays the integrator and signs the contract with it, and often has no contract with the vendor at all beyond the license agreement. Sometimes a distributor sits between the vendor and the integrator, holding license stock, extending credit to partners and invoicing them. Vigel Antonov, head of the technical center at the distributor Merlion, says the Russian market is moving towards a mature "vendor → distributor → integrator" chain. In a Merlion survey of fifty organizations, 74.5% said that a pilot in real conditions makes them more likely to choose a Russian product, and integrators, according to Merlion, spend up to a year choosing a solution.

To understand why an integrator picks one vendor over another, count its money. Forrester analyst Jay McBain wrote in 2021 that large cloud vendors count between $4.65 and $9.00 of opportunity for partners around every dollar of their own sales, in hardware, software and services, and professional services make up almost two thirds of that. An IDC study commissioned by Microsoft shows a similar picture: for every dollar of Microsoft revenue, Microsoft's managed service partners made $8.45. The conclusion for a vendor is plain: the license discount matters less to an integrator than the work it can sell around your software, and the risk that this work goes wrong.

Risk costs the integrator money too. Vendors require certified engineers from partners: GitLab's Professional Services Partner status needs at least three certified engineers and an average of two registered services sold with GitLab licenses per quarter, the Russian vendor Galaktika requires certified consultants on staff at every partner tier, and SUSE's Emerald tier requires two certifications. The integrator pays for the training itself. If its engineers already know a competitor's platform and not yours, the first migration to your platform is more expensive and riskier for it, even when your license is cheaper.

<!-- section: theory | Deal registration and what it protects -->
Deal registration is a partner's claim to the vendor: "I found this opportunity at this customer and I am working it." The vendor reviews the claim and, if it approves, reserves the opportunity for the partner for a period and gives it an extra discount. The rules at large vendors are similar, and they are worth knowing, because an integrator will compare your terms with them.

GitLab's public handbook says an approved registration lasts 90 days from approval, a 30-day extension is at GitLab's discretion, and GitLab must respond to the partner within two business days. Approval is based on order of receipt, qualification of the opportunity, the partner's ability to support the customer in its country and the partner's relationship with the customer. Only one partner per opportunity earns the higher sourced-deal discount, and flat renewals are not eligible. Sysdig's published terms reserve a deal for six months with up to three months' extension at Sysdig's discretion, approve one partner per opportunity, state that registration does not guarantee a particular margin, and remove the registration if the partner stops actively pursuing the deal. Red Hat promises not to bring competing partners into a registered deal itself and gives increased margin and price protection for registration.

Russian practice works the same way, only stricter about inactivity. In the CICADA8 partner program for 2026, a project is reserved for a partner for no more than two months, and the partner must update the status of its work with the customer at least once every two weeks; a 30-day extension is at the vendor's discretion. If the partner does nothing on the project for a month, if the customer complains about its actions, or if the customer reports that it buys only from a closed list of suppliers without this partner, the vendor may remove the registration. If identical requests arrive within one working day, the project is declared open to all partners who filed that day, and if the customer itself asked several partners for quotes at the same time, the vendor may register the project to several partners at once. A partner may run a pilot at a customer only for a registered project. Cleverence promises partners that even if a client comes to the vendor directly, it will be sent to the partner to buy licenses.

Note that registration protects one opportunity at one customer for a limited time; it does not reserve the whole customer for the partner. Buyers know the other side of this too. NPI, a firm that helps companies buy hardware and software, explains to clients that by approaching one reseller first, the customer creates a sole source for itself: other resellers struggle to match the price, and the manufacturer is reluctant to remove the registration, so the customer sometimes has to wait until it expires.

<!-- section: theory | The case: the first six weeks -->
The names in the case are made up. The vendor is Northern Stack, an open platform that runs virtual machines and Kubernetes on the customer's own hardware. Sales are run by the founder, Anna. The customer is Prioksky Bank, where the deal was opened by Igor, the head of infrastructure. The integrator is Sigma, a VMware partner for twelve years with a framework agreement with the bank for hardware, software and support. At Sigma, Pavel is the account manager for the bank, and Dina is the presales engineer doing the technical work.

At the first meeting Igor tells Anna: "Our VMware renewal got more expensive, and we are looking for a replacement for two hundred virtual machines. But our procurement will not sign with a small vendor, everything goes through Sigma." This is the first decision point. Anna could try to persuade the bank to buy directly, but the bank's procurement rule is not hers to change, and arguing with it means losing time. She does the right thing: she asks Igor to introduce her to Pavel himself, so the integrator hears about the deal from its own client, whom it trusts.

On the call Pavel asks three questions: what discount Sigma will get, whether Northern Stack will register the deal for Sigma, and who will fix the platform if it fails at night. Northern Stack has no partner program. Anna promises "about twenty percent, we'll work it out", writes in an email "we are reserving the bank for you", and says her engineer will answer at night. This is the second decision point, and Anna makes three mistakes. The email does not say for how long and for which deal the reservation holds or what removes it. The discount is named without knowing how much Sigma will earn on the migration and spend on training Dina. Night support is promised without agreeing who takes the call first.

For the next six weeks Sigma is silent. Pavel answers every email with "next week". Later it turns out that Dina was moved to a large government tender, and Pavel meanwhile showed the bank a competitor's platform: Sigma is a high-tier partner of that competitor, has three certified engineers on it and a registration discount, and knows how to sell a migration to it without risk. Anna learns this from Igor. This is the third decision point. Taking offence at the integrator is pointless: it chooses what earns more with less risk, exactly as the sources above describe. Anna asks Pavel for a one-hour meeting with the agenda "joint plan before the pilot" and brings an offer: free training for Dina, a Northern Stack engineer on the pilot at the vendor's expense, all migration work and first-line support stay with Sigma, and the registration is confirmed in an email with a date, a 90-day term and an obligation to update the status every two weeks.

<!-- section: theory | The case: a second partner and a direct call -->
In week seven the bank's procurement, following its own rule, requests three quotes. It asks for one of them from Technomarket, a reseller that works through a large distributor. Technomarket's manager writes to Anna: "We are ready to take a smaller markup, give us a better price than Sigma." This is the fourth decision point. If Anna gives Technomarket the same discount, Sigma will lose the tender to a company that did not spend a single hour on the pilot, and will never bring Northern Stack another customer. Anna answers the way GitLab's rules for multi-bid tenders say: the higher discount goes to the partner that sourced the deal and worked with the customer before the price request, and the other bidders get the base partner price. The bank's procurement is unhappy about the price difference. Anna explains to Igor that the difference pays for Sigma's work on the pilot and migration, and that any partner who had done that work first would have received the same discount.

In week eight Igor calls Anna himself: "Sigma is dragging its feet, let's buy from you directly through a procurement exception, it's better for you anyway." This is the fifth decision point, and the temptation is strong: the integrator's margin stays with the vendor, and the deal speeds up. Anna refuses and immediately tells Pavel about the call. Her reasons are practical. Sigma supports all of the bank's hardware and will stay after signing, so without Sigma the platform would sit at the customer without first-line support. Every future integrator would learn that Northern Stack takes deals away, and would not come. Bypassing a partner can also be expensive: in 2008 the reseller Infra-Comm sued Cisco for passing its registered opportunity to AT&T, the jury awarded Infra-Comm $6.4 million, and Cisco settled for $5.5 million. After Anna's call, Pavel puts Dina back on the deal.

<!-- section: theory | The case: pilot, signing and what went wrong -->
The pilot lasts five weeks. Before it starts, the parties write down who does what. Sigma installs the platform on the bank's hardware, migrates the test virtual machines and takes all of the bank's requests as first line. Northern Stack handles second and third line, meaning it investigates complex failures and ships fixes, and its engineer joins night calls only through Dina. The bank writes the pilot success criteria itself: migrating twenty production machines, recovering after a node outage, and the time it takes to upgrade the platform. Five months after the first meeting, the bank signs a contract with Sigma for licenses for two of the three sites it planned; it postponed the third to next year because part of this year's budget had already gone on the VMware renewal.

Looking back at what went wrong, the mistakes sit in the first two weeks. The registration was verbal, so Sigma was not sure the vendor would not go to the bank directly and did not commit its people to the deal. The discount was named before talking about the integrator's money, and those twenty percent did not outweigh a ready-made migration service for the competitor's platform. Sigma had no joint plan with dates and a named engineer, so Dina was easily taken for another project. Anna made up the price for a second partner at the moment it was asked, although the bank is obliged to request three quotes every time. Most importantly, Anna did not ask on the first call which other platforms Sigma works with and at what partner tier. That question comes first, because an integrator almost always sells your competitors too.

<!-- section: theory | When the vendor goes to the customer itself -->
Large vendors bypass partners too. In a 2012 survey of 150 partners reported by The Register, a third of respondents said a vendor had rejected their registration and then its own salespeople took the opportunity, and a quarter said vendors later used registration records for direct sales. After acquiring VMware, Broadcom announced that it would work directly with its two thousand largest customers, and in December 2024 it cut that list to five hundred and handed fifteen hundred companies back to partners, adding fifteen percent of deal value to fund professional services; a Canalys analyst called it a U-turn. Competitors respond to such moves in their own way: while VMware partners were unsettled, Nutanix raised its deal registration discounts and added service rebates for partners. In Russia, a partner survey at PROWAY Security Day 2024 showed that fourteen percent were unhappy with distributors over deal protection problems, and a representative of Kod Bezopasnosti names a distributor's participation in direct sales to customers as a reason partners switch distributors.

The rule that removes most of these conflicts is best written down in advance. GitLab puts it this way: on renewals, GitLab salespeople must work with the partner that made the most recent sale unless the customer asks in writing to work without it, and GitLab does not offer a direct renewal quote when there is such a partner. Broadcom also introduced incumbency protection on renewals for VMware partners, and Sysdig gives registration benefits on a renewal only to the incumbent partner.

<!-- section: theory | Cloud marketplaces as one more channel -->
Large customers increasingly buy software through cloud marketplaces, because that way they spend an annual budget already committed to the cloud. On AWS, the vendor gives the partner a wholesale price, the partner adds its markup and issues an offer to a single buyer; AWS deducts a fee from the vendor, which for a private offer under one million dollars is three percent plus another half percent if the deal goes through a partner. Google charges no additional fee for selling through a partner and leaves invoicing to the partner. At Microsoft, a purchase counts towards the customer's Azure spending commitment only if the software runs in Azure: a license deployed on the customer's own hardware or in a hybrid setup does not count. For a platform that runs on the customer's hardware, this buyer argument therefore usually does not apply, and the integrator remains the main route.

<!-- section: task | Assignment: the plan for your deal through an integrator -->
Take a deal that is already running, or will soon start, through an integrator, reseller or distributor. If you do not have one, take a tier A company from your Dream 100 list and find out who it buys infrastructure through: in public procurement this is visible from the suppliers of past contracts, and in conversation the easiest way is to ask an engineer.

Fill in the form below, which has eleven fields. Start with the customer's procurement rule and the integrator, then count the integrator's money: what discount it will get, what services it will sell around your software and what it will spend on training. Write the registration terms the way you would send them to the partner in an email: date, term, update frequency and what removes the registration. Decide in advance what price a second partner who comes with the same customer will get, and what you will answer if the customer asks to buy from you directly. Spell out who runs the pilot and migration and who handles first, second and third line support. Make a joint plan with dates up to signing and a named person at the integrator for every step. Separately, write down which other platforms the integrator works with and at what partner tier: this is the question Anna did not ask on the first call. In the last two fields, write what you will do if the integrator goes silent for two weeks and what signs will tell you it is pushing a competitor.

Check yourself before sending the terms to the partner: the registration has a term and a removal condition; every step of the plan has a date and a person at the integrator; the division of work has no line where both sides or neither side is responsible.

? An integrator asks the vendor for a bigger discount than the competitor gives. What should the vendor find out first?
- Whether the integrator will commit to a three-year minimum volume || A volume commitment does not explain why the integrator prefers the competitor's platform.
+ How much the integrator will earn on services around the platform and spend on training || Most of an integrator's money comes from services, and the decision depends on them.
- What discount the competitor gives other partners in this region || Someone else's discount does not show how much this integrator earns on the deal.

? What does an approved deal registration protect in programs like GitLab's and Sysdig's?
+ One opportunity at one customer for a term the vendor may extend || That is what both vendors' terms say: 90 days at GitLab and six months at Sysdig.
- All future customers in the partner's region while its partner agreement with the vendor is in force || Registration is for one opportunity; it does not reserve a region.
- All sales to this customer for the whole contract term, including future flat renewals || GitLab states plainly that flat renewals are not eligible for registration.

? The customer requests three quotes, and a second reseller asks for a lower price than the partner that ran the pilot. What should the vendor do?
+ Keep the higher discount for the partner that sourced the deal, give the rest the base price || This is GitLab's rule for tenders, and it keeps the partner's reason to invest in pilots.
- Give both partners the same price so the customer can pick the best contractor on the quality of work || Then the partner that invested in the pilot loses to one that did no work, and will not bring another deal.
- Refuse to quote the second reseller and tell the customer the project is already registered to the first partner || The customer must collect three quotes, and a refusal will break its procurement.

? The customer's head of infrastructure offers to buy directly from the vendor because the integrator is slow. Which answer makes more sense?
+ Decline, tell the partner about the call and propose a three-way meeting || First-line support stays with the integrator, and bypassing a partner drives away the whole channel.
- Agree, if the customer finds an exception in its procurement rules itself || The partner will keep supporting the customer's hardware and will learn about the bypass.
- Agree and give the integrator part of the margin as compensation for the lost deal || Compensation will not restore trust, and the integrator will stop investing in your deals.

+ A month of partner inactivity or a customer complaint about its actions on the project || Both cases are listed explicitly in the program among the grounds for removing a registration.
- The customer gaining a second division with its own budget for buying hardware and licences || The terms say nothing about this.
- The partner refusing to run a pilot at its own expense || Pilots are allowed only for registered projects, but refusing a pilot does not remove the registration.
