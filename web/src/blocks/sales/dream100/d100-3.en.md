<!-- section: hook -->
A founder sent a bank's head of infrastructure a LinkedIn invitation without a note, a work email the next day, a LinkedIn message the day after that, then another email, and called the company's main number on the fifth day. The manager never replied, and a week later LinkedIn restricted the founder's invitations. Another founder found a talk by an engineer from the same bank about the difficulty of upgrading customer clusters. He sent the engineer one email with a rollback test plan addressing that problem and a short question asking whether the plan would be useful to the person responsible for the work. The engineer forwarded the email to the manager. This lesson explains how to combine communication channels into a cadence that respects a person's time, and the platform rules and laws behind it.

<!-- section: theory | A three-week sequence -->
An outreach sequence starts with preparation. Choose one company problem, two buying committee roles, and a useful resource: a KubeVirt migration checklist, a tenant isolation test plan, or a calculator with explicit hardware and support assumptions. Look for a warm introduction first. Write observations and assumptions separately: “Your public talk mentions X; we assumed Y is still difficult.” Do not pretend to know the company's private architecture.

In the first four days, send one short, truthful email: the company's problem, one useful result or resource, a small question, and the required sender and unsubscribe information. If the person has a public post on the topic, reply substantively, without concealed advertising. A LinkedIn invitation is appropriate only when there is an honest professional reason for it. Liking three posts before sending a message is pointless: nobody has measured such an effect.

On the seventh to tenth day, send one follow-up that adds something new: a reproducible failure test, a case study of a similar operator, or an answer to an open question. If you are already connected on LinkedIn and the person finds it convenient, you can send a short voice message with a text summary. Where lawful and appropriate, a call to a work number can establish who owns the problem. Do not email, call, and message the same person on the same day to force a reply.

On the fourteenth to twenty-first day, suggest a conversation at an upcoming event or write a short email saying that you will stop contacting them, and stop. Resume only after a significant new trigger or on an agreed date. If someone else owns the problem, write to that person with relevant context; do not surround the first person with colleagues receiving identical emails. A refusal or unsubscribe cancels every sequence.

Example email: “Your team's public talk about operating customer clusters raised the issue of upgrades when each customer has a different configuration. We are building an open-source cloud platform and have written a plan for testing rollback and tenant isolation. Would this plan be useful to whoever is responsible for this work at your company? If this is not relevant to you, let me know and I will stop contacting you.” Add the legal company name, address, and an unsubscribe method. Send it only if the talk you mention exists and the address was obtained lawfully. The example shows the tone and structure of the email; whether it may be sent to a particular person depends on the country and is checked separately, as described in the assignment.

Kubegrade, a small cluster upgrade project, selected 113 developers who had worked with similar open-source tools in the preceding 180 days, contacted 112 through LinkedIn invitations and messages followed by emails, and received 25 replies and five demo requests. This was a search for feedback before the product's release, sometimes with compensation, with no control group and no revenue.

<!-- section: theory | LinkedIn rules -->
LinkedIn limits invitations for all members, including paid subscribers, but does not publish an exact weekly number; advice such as “a hundred a week” or “two hundred with Sales Navigator” is not an official rule. A restriction after frequent rejections usually lasts a week; withdrawing unanswered invitations does not lift it, and buying Premium does not grant more invitations during the restriction. A free account can add a note of up to 200 characters to only a few invitations per month; one help page says three, another says five, so check what your account displays. Sales Navigator provides 50 InMail messages per month to people you are not connected with. Voice messages are recorded in the mobile app, last up to a minute, and can be sent only to existing connections.

LinkedIn's User Agreement prohibits bots and unauthorized automation for accessing the service, collecting contacts, sending messages, and artificially increasing activity. Low volumes, random pauses, and a software vendor's assurances that a tool is “safe” do not make automation authorized. In January 2025, LinkedIn sued Proxycurl for scraping data through fake accounts, and in July 2025 announced that the case had been settled.

If people do not accept invitations, find other routes: a mutual contact can ask permission to introduce you, an investor or advisor can identify who actually owns the problem, a public technical discussion can demonstrate your usefulness, and a meetup talk or partner introduction can create a natural meeting. Groups, Slack communities, alumni networks, and events provide a place to participate, but not consent to a private sales approach. Do not bypass restrictions with new accounts or repeatedly withdraw and resend invitations.

<!-- section: theory | Cold email -->
Gmail's sender requirements, covered in [the lesson on your own newsletter](#/lesson/marketing.email-newsletter) of the marketing course, also apply to a founder's cold email. Every sender must configure SPF or DKIM, encryption, and correct DNS records; bulk senders need both SPF and DKIM, DMARC with alignment, and one-click unsubscribe. Configure the full set even for low email volumes: these rules do not mean anyone wants unsolicited email. A separate outreach domain does not legalize spam, and subdomains do not bypass Gmail's bulk-sender threshold. Lookalike domains that conceal the sender and the practice of “burning a domain and starting a new one” are poor practices. Send from an identifiable address that accepts replies, at a steady, low volume, investigate bounces, and stop when complaints arise.

Cold email reply rates are low. Belkins analyzed 7,5 million client emails sent in 2025 and counted 0,45 replies per hundred emails sent, or 0,22 for companies with more than ten thousand employees; replies include refusals and are not meetings. Mailshake reported in 2025 that most campaigns had reply rates from 1 to 4 %, but uses a different denominator, so these figures cannot be compared. In your own work, count emails sent, unique people reached by delivered email, positive replies, and completed meetings separately.

<!-- section: theory | Phone, video, and gifts -->
A short call to a work number can help establish who owns a problem or confirm a meeting. Record the outcome and any request not to call again, and do not search for a personal number because an email went unanswered. Business-to-business calls are also regulated: in 2024, the FTC extended its prohibition on telemarketing misrepresentations to sales to businesses.

A one- to two-minute Loom or Vidyard video with captions explaining one architecture diagram is appropriate after a person has shown interest; check whether the video leads to meetings, which view counts will not tell you. A gift such as a book, lunch at a workshop, or a printed technical guide is appropriate when a relationship already exists: ask whether the company allows gifts and where to send them. Do not look up home addresses through enrichment services, and do not make a gift conditional on a purchase. Ask banks and government organizations about their gift policies.

<!-- section: theory | Laws -->
In the US, CAN-SPAM applies to commercial email between businesses. The email must have truthful headers and a truthful subject line, be identified as advertising, include the sender's postal address, and provide a clear unsubscribe method; unsubscribe requests must be honored within ten business days, and the mechanism must work for at least thirty days after sending. Unsubscribe people immediately rather than waiting for the deadline. In the European Union, outreach based on legitimate interest requires an assessment: whether the purpose is lawful, whether data processing is necessary, whether the person's rights outweigh it, and what the person can reasonably expect. Business email is not exempt from the rules, and national electronic marketing laws apply alongside the GDPR. A person's objection to direct marketing ends processing of their data for that purpose without further balancing. In Canada, CASL requires express consent, applicable implied consent, or an exception; a published address implies consent only if it is not accompanied by a statement prohibiting advertising and the email relates to the person's role. A GitHub profile is not blanket permission to email.

<!-- section: task | Assignment: one sequence for one company -->
The work happens in your Dream 100 spreadsheet from the first lesson of the module. Choose one tier A company and one person from its buying committee.

Before writing the emails, check whether you may email this person, using five questions, and record the answers next to the draft:

1. Where the address came from. Record the source: the company’s page, a signature in a public email, an introduction. An address from a purchased list, or one guessed from a pattern without verification, is ruled out at this step.
2. Which country the person and their company are in and which rules apply: CAN-SPAM in the US, the GDPR together with the national electronic marketing law in the European Union, CASL in Canada. If the country is not in the Laws section, find out its rules before sending, not after.
3. What basis the email has under these rules. For the European Union, record the legitimate-interest assessment using the four questions from the Laws section; for Canada, consent or applicable implied consent: the address is published without a statement prohibiting advertising, and the email relates to the person’s role.
4. Whether the person or address is on the Do not contact sheet.
5. Whether the email has the legal name, a postal address and a working unsubscribe method, and whether SPF, DKIM and DMARC are set up as in [the lesson on your own newsletter](#/lesson/marketing.email-newsletter).

If any question has no answer, do not send the email. If your company has a lawyer or someone responsible for data protection, show them the answers to the second and third questions before the first send to a new country. Find public material from that person or their team that gives you an honest reason to write. Write the first email using this lesson's example, a follow-up on the tenth day with new material, and a closing email on the twenty-first day. Record the dates, channels, and conditions that stop the sequence in the activities table.
