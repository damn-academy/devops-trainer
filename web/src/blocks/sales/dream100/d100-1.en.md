<!-- section: hook -->
A founder made a list of a hundred companies he wanted as customers and spent six months emailing, calling, and attending conferences. By autumn, he had four hundred contacts in three places: a spreadsheet, email, and LinkedIn. He sent the same cold email twice to someone he had already met, missed the fact that his main supporter at one bank had moved to another bank on the same list, and could not remember whom he had promised a test plan. A Dream 100 program depends on recordkeeping. This lesson explains how to organize it: which tables you need, how to divide companies into tiers, how to record relationship stages, and how to work out what to do next in forty-five minutes once a week.

<!-- section: theory | What is Dream 100 -->
Dream 100 is a program in which a company selects a hundred organizations that best fit its customer profile and works with them personally over an extended period, instead of sending proposals to thousands of unfamiliar recipients. For an infrastructure product, these include banks, telecom operators, hosting providers, companies moving away from VMware, and those building GPU clouds. A hundred companies are a lot for one person, so the program divides them into tiers and actively works with only a small subset. PostHog, for example, assigns its technical salespeople cold outreach to ten accounts at a time and handles the rest in other ways.

<!-- section: theory | Eight tables -->
A practical way to organize records is to use eight small tables, with a permanent ID for each record.

The companies table stores the domain and its variants, legal name, parent and subsidiary relationships, country and sites, segment, owner, tier, reasons the company is a fit, disqualifying factors, relationship stage and the date it entered that stage, first and latest substantive interaction, next action with a date, and a do-not-contact flag. For an infrastructure product, add the current virtualization platform, owned or rented hardware, deployment model, tenancy model, regulatory requirements, renewal or migration deadline, and who verified each of these claims. Leave unknowns blank: “uses VMware” does not mean “has approved moving away from VMware.”

The people table stores the name, public professional profile, permitted work contact details, role, time zone, language, preferred contact method, and verification date. A person's employment belongs in a separate employment table: person, company, job title, start and end dates, source, and observation date. When someone changes jobs, add a new record and retain the old one, because the old company loses a supporter and the new company gains someone you know. A work email address belongs to the employment record and ends with that employment.

The buying committee table links people to a specific company initiative and, later, to a sales opportunity. Its roles are: the engineer who will operate the system; technical evaluator; internal supporter, known as a champion; platform owner; economic buyer with budget authority; security and compliance specialist; procurement and legal; and opponent. One person can hold several roles, and several people can share a role. For each person, record their priorities, evidence of influence, attitude toward your proposal, and someone who could introduce you. An enthusiastic engineer is not yet a champion if they have no influence within the company.

Use a scale from zero to four to record relationship strength: zero means “person identified,” one means “some interaction,” two means “a substantive two-way conversation,” three means “willingly helps with context or introductions,” and four means “advocates for you inside their company.” Store evidence and a date alongside the score, and permission to cite this person in a separate field. An accepted LinkedIn invitation, an entry in an investor's address book, or a GitHub star does not bring a relationship to level three.

The other four tables are signals, activities, opportunities, and contact suppression. The signals table is append-only: type, source link, a short factual excerpt, event date, discovery date, confidence, method used to match it to a company and person, review date, proposed significance, and who assessed it. Store facts and interpretations in separate fields: a job posting proves that a company is looking for a Kubernetes engineer, not that it has a platform budget. The activities table stores direction, channel, person, company, assignee, time, outcome, commitments, and next step. Create an opportunity record only when the buyer has confirmed an initiative and agreed to the next evaluation; a software download is not an opportunity. Check the suppression table before every send from any tool, and never let a data update reactivate someone who has opted out of email.

<!-- section: theory | Tiers and stages -->
Start by dividing the hundred companies into three tiers: ten tier A companies, each requiring individual research; thirty tier B companies, grouped by a common problem; and sixty tier C companies that you monitor for changes. Review tiers monthly. A hosting provider belongs in tier A when there is evidence of a commercially significant initiative and a real path to the right people; being well known is not a reason. Keep large companies with internal platforms, regional hosting providers, and partners in separate views: their purchasing economics differ.

Company relationship stages are defined by evidence. “Unaware” means there is no evidence of awareness. “Aware” means someone at the company recognized you or attended your event. “Engaged” means there has been a substantive two-way conversation about a problem that matters to them. “Meeting” means a qualification meeting has taken place; record scheduled meetings separately. “Opportunity” means the buyer has confirmed an initiative and agreed on the next evaluation or decision. You also need “monitoring,” “not a fit,” “won,” and “lost” stages. An ad impression, an email open, or a website visit attributed by address does not move a company to the next stage.

<!-- section: theory | Monday in forty-five minutes -->
Run a weekly review in the same order. First, resolve overdue commitments and email opt-outs. Then review changes at tier A companies and gaps in their buying committees. Select a few companies with a new reason to act, and assign an owner and date to each action. Finally, review last week's completed meetings and new opportunities. You do not need to read every row during the review. Create saved views for this: “active with no next step,” “only one person known,” “champion left,” “trigger unverified,” and “meeting held, no buyer decision.”

Keep data orderly: deduplicate using permanent IDs and verified domains, record subsidiaries explicitly, store dates in UTC and the meeting's local time separately, check a person's employment before every email, investigate bounced emails, flag stale fields, and record where enriched data came from.

Measure the number of companies researched, engaged, met with, converted into opportunities, and won; the number of buying committee roles covered; the share of scheduled meetings that took place; the share of completed meetings that became opportunities; days in stage; overdue steps; and hours and money per opportunity. Count positive replies separately from all replies. For small groups, show counts such as “3 out of 12” alongside percentages.

<!-- section: theory | Spreadsheet or CRM -->
For a solo founder who maintains records daily, one spreadsheet workbook with eight sheets, protected IDs, dropdown lists, next-action views, and a daily backup is enough. Move to a CRM when shared access, permissions, change history, or synchronization start causing missed actions; there is no magic company count at which a CRM becomes necessary.

Railway, a cloud platform for developers, uses Attio to track customer workspaces, usage, and revenue, with an adoption score calculated from product data. The company had previously tried other CRMs but could not represent its usage-based pricing model in them, so signals were getting lost in chats. HubSpot's target-account features, such as ideal customer profile tier and buying role, are available only on paid Professional and Enterprise plans. In Pipedrive, companies are organizations, people are contacts, next steps are activities, and a deal is created only after qualification. Clay serves as a research and data enrichment layer that writes to one primary CRM; start with ten rows, require a source link for every fact, and do not let automation overwrite fields a human has verified.

<!-- section: task | Assignment: one company, three people and one next action -->
Complete the assignment in the Dream 100 template downloadable on this page. Row two of every sheet contains a worked example: the fictional hosting provider Westhost, account A001, three people P001–P003, their employment, buying-center roles and one next action. Notice how account and person IDs recur in Employment, Buying center and Activities: those IDs link the records.

First, do the same for one of your own tier A companies. Fill its Companies row: ID, domain, tier, reason for fit, stage and next action with a date are required; other fields may remain blank. Find three people in different roles, record them in People, their employment in Employment and their buying-center roles in Buying center, including relationship strength on the lesson’s scale and evidence. Record one next action in Activities.

Check your work without AI: the company has a dated next action, every person has a role and evidence of relationship strength, and account and person IDs match across all sheets. Once the first company is complete, add the other nine tier A companies the same way. Create the “only one person known” and “active without a next step” views with filters: count people per account in Buying center, and filter Companies for empty next actions.
