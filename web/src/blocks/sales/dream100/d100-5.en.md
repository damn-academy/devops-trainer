<!-- lesson: sales.d100-inperson | In-person meetings: conferences, offices, and meetups | 22 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
A founder flew across the ocean to KubeCon, spent three days moving between booths and talks, and returned with a stack of business cards and two substantive conversations. A year later he went again, but five weeks before the conference he compared his company list with the speakers and sponsors, wrote to twelve people with a specific question for each, and offered two short time slots. Nine meetings took place, and after two of them engineers invited him to run a working session at their offices. This lesson explains how to prepare for in-person meetings at conferences, how to arrange a visit to a company's office, and how to find local communities in US cities.

<!-- section: theory | A conference plan, week by week -->
Nobody legally sells a list of all conference attendees: at KubeCon, sponsors receive contact details only for people whose badges were scanned at their booths. Rely on the public schedule, speaker biographies, permitted networking tools in the conference app, and people who have publicly announced their attendance. Treat offers to buy the full attendee list as suspicious.

Six to three weeks before the event, compare your company list with speakers, sponsors, public travel announcements, and your contacts. Offer each person two short slots tied to a specific question; record who accepted, declined, or did not respond. Book travel and meeting rooms around a reasonable meeting plan. From two weeks before the event through the event day, send each person the purpose, location, duration, and attendees, confirm the meeting the day before, and allow time to walk between venues.

After a talk or at someone else's booth, start briefly: “Your point about upgrading customer clusters was useful. We are building a platform in this area; may I ask one technical question?” Do not block the queue or interrupt someone else's conversation, and ask permission to continue later. Within two business days, send what you promised, briefly describe the problem you discussed, and propose only the next step you agreed on.

At KubeCon North America 2025, booths collected 90 571 scans, averaging 288 per booth; these are scans, with far fewer buyers behind them. In October 2025, PostHog wrote that fifteen events per quarter and a budget of 10 000 dollars per month seemed right for meeting champions and customers and learning, and that cooking classes with workshops, breakfasts, founder conversations, and talks were worth repeating. Calculate your trip's cost as follows: admission, flights, hotel, food, sponsorship, production, and founder hours, divided by completed meetings with companies that fit your ideal customer profile. A 6000-dollar trip with six such meetings costs 1000 dollars per meeting, and if two meetings become opportunities, each costs 3000 dollars, excluding salary.

<!-- section: theory | Visiting a company's office -->
PostHog describes customer visits as follows: visit customers who pay or are likely to pay at least 50 000 dollars a year and have an expansion, renewal, or technical or organizational obstacle. The salesperson prepares the visit without burdening the customer's champion. Formats include a one-hour working session, a 30–60-minute conversation with the champion, and a 30-minute executive summary.

You can request a visit like this: “I will be in your city from [date] to [date]. If your platform team would like to examine the upgrade and rollback problem we discussed, I can bring our maintainer for a one-hour working session. We will prepare the agenda and test environment. Would that be useful, and who should we invite?” Offer remote participation and a neutral venue if few people go to the office. A meeting with executives addresses cost, schedule risks, and responsibility for operations, while a session with engineers should end with a test or a decision.

US offices host agreed technical events with an internal host and registration; an uninvited vendor will not be admitted. At the Kubernetes training day at Microsoft's NERD center in Boston in September 2025, attendees had to register with their full names and show ID, and there were 40 places even though the registration platform listed a capacity of 1000. For a lunchtime talk at a customer's office, agree on the internal host, topic and outcome, guest registration, security requirements, room and equipment, link for remote participants, participant roles, and permitted food, and run the demo in a training environment.

<!-- section: theory | Meetups in US cities -->
After the platform migration, look for local CNCF communities in the Open Community Groups directory, as well as on Meetup and in Luma calendars, using Kubernetes, platform engineering, SRE, OpenStack, KubeVirt, and cloud native as search terms. Check recent meetings and actual upcoming dates. Cloud Native San Francisco met at the offices of Sysdig, GitHub, and Reddit in 2025–2026; Cloud Native NYC welcomes speakers, demos, sponsors, and volunteers and has held meetings at the New York Times; Cloud Native Seattle also meets in Bellevue, where one venue escorts guests through the building; KCD Texas 2025 took place at Austin's Central Library; Cloud Native Boston has run hands-on sessions, including on KubeVirt, and a session in September 2025 cost 40 dollars; Cloud Native DC is organized by the KCD DC team, with meetings in Arlington. Search separately for neighboring cities within each metropolitan area and verify addresses before scheduling back-to-back meetings.

A meetup talk is the most straightforward way to meet engineers from local companies. Write to the organizer with an abstract, takeaways for the audience, a reproducible demo, and your employer, and offer to discuss a failure or tradeoff. Ask permission to share slides and collect voluntary requests to continue the conversation, but never ask for the group's membership list as payment for a talk.

<!-- section: task | Assignment: a meeting plan for the next conference -->
Reading the lesson takes about twenty minutes; the meeting plan takes several evenings before the conference. The work happens in your Dream 100 spreadsheet from the first lesson of the module. Take the next conference your buyers attend and compare its public schedule and sponsor list with your tier A and B companies. Choose ten people and record a question worth meeting about, two time slots, and a contact method for each. This week, start with three people from tier A companies and add the other seven before you travel. For one company you already have a relationship with, write a request for an office working session using this lesson's example.

<!-- lesson: sales.d100-us | Moving to the US and building your first contacts from scratch | 22 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
A European infrastructure company founder decided to move to San Francisco because “all the deals are there.” Three months later, he discovered that the platform teams of two of his main US buyers were in Europe, the third was in Texas, his visa was not ready yet, and living in the city cost 7500 dollars a month, excluding salary. Another founder first visited for a month with meetings booked in advance, checked where the decision-makers actually worked, and moved only when three companies requested further working sessions. This lesson covers what being physically present in the US changes, how to test a relocation decision before making it, and how to establish initial contacts when you have almost no network in the country.

<!-- section: theory | What being present offers -->
There are no published measurements of how much a founder's relocation increases an infrastructure company's sales. There are founders' observations. In February 2025, Plain's European founders opened a San Francisco office alongside a 15-million-dollar Series A to be closer to their growing customer base. PostHog's founders once moved to San Francisco for the Y Combinator accelerator to access its dense founder network, but did not intend to stay permanently for family reasons. In a study by Sifted and AVP, which in 2025 surveyed more than 250 founders from Europe and the US, Nabla's cofounder says she received more offers of help and coffee meetings in New York, but had to wait six months for a visa.

These observations suggest a testable hypothesis: being in the country makes repeat meetings, customer working sessions, partner meetings, and events announced a couple of days in advance easier. Test it against the locations of decision-makers at your target companies; a headquarters address tells you little here. A US company's platform team may be in Europe, and a hosting provider may be far from the coast.

<!-- section: theory | How to test the decision before moving -->
Before moving, map buyers' and partners' locations, visit for a month with conversations booked in advance, check visa feasibility, calculate housing, health insurance, and travel costs, and decide who will cover your responsibilities in Europe. A reasonable test before deciding is three companies requesting repeat working sessions and two partners ready to work together; these numbers are examples, not a rule.

A business trip does not authorize you to work. The US Department of State permits contract negotiations, consultations, and conferences on a B-1 visa and distinguishes them from employment in the US; a conference invitation, a US company, a coworking desk, and an investor do not grant work authorization. Options for a founder include O-1A for people with extraordinary ability, L-1 for employee transfers between related companies, E-2 for investors from treaty countries, and International Entrepreneur Parole, each with its own conditions; review them with an immigration lawyer using official pages.

An illustrative monthly budget in a major US city is: housing and utilities 3500 dollars, food and local travel 1200, workspace 350, insurance and paperwork 700, intercity travel and events 1500, and sales software 250. That is 7500 dollars per month and 22 500 for three months, excluding salary, taxes, a lawyer, deposits, and family expenses. These are planning assumptions, and each line should be replaced with a quote for your city. A coworking service such as WeWork provides desks for the day and limited meeting-room time, while private founder dinners, such as the Unusual Ventures and PostHog dinner in June 2026, require approval to attend.

In the first 90 days, choose one primary city, attend one regular practitioner community, group working sessions and partner meetings geographically, and leave time for follow-up and overlap with the European team. On days 30, 60, and 90, compare against the situation before the move: new buying committee relationships, completed meetings with companies that fit your profile, repeat invitations, product evaluations, and actual expenses. Networking activity alone does not justify relocation.

<!-- section: theory | A double opt-in warm introduction -->
A double opt-in warm introduction works like this. Find someone who knows the person you want to meet, explain a specific technical reason, and make it easy to decline. Send a short email they can forward. They privately ask the other person whether they want an introduction and introduce you only if both agree. Record who made the introduction, to whom, when you asked, the outcome, and whether permission was given.

A forwardable email could read: “Alex is building an open-source cloud platform on Kubernetes. Your team's public migration talk overlaps with his work on tenant isolation and upgrade rollback. Alex can share a reproducible test plan and would welcome a twenty-minute technical comparison if useful. There is no need to reply if this is outside your area.” Adjust the roles and claims to match the facts, include one evidence link, and do not make the intermediary retell your presentation.

Send an investor or advisor a short list of five companies with exact roles, a hypothesis about the problem, what you can contribute, and why the intermediary might know these people. Ask them to assess the strength of their relationship before requesting an introduction. “That is not the right person; talk to operations” is also progress, even though it is not a sales opportunity.

<!-- section: theory | Marketplaces, integrators, and talks -->
AWS ISV Accelerate is a co-selling program for software that runs on or integrates with AWS and is sold through AWS Marketplace. Participation requires a public marketplace listing, participation in the ACE program, and, among other things, five launched and fifteen qualified opportunities in ACE over the past 12 months. A marketplace listing alone does not provide introductions to AWS salespeople. AWS cites a Forrester model in which marketplace deals close twice as fast and are four to five times larger, but the model is based on interviews with fourteen partners. For a private cloud platform on owned hardware, first check whether a cloud marketplace suits your buyer; an integrator or regional hosting provider may be a more natural fit. Agree with an integrator in advance on who sells migration services, who supports the production system, who owns the relationship and escalation, how revenue is shared, and whether the partner can introduce you to companies with the customer's permission. Start with one joint workshop or evaluation.

If you have no network at all, spend a month as follows: choose a narrow buyer problem, find two active communities focused on it, create one useful resource, submit one practical talk, request a few double opt-in introductions, hold conversations, and revisit your buyer hypothesis.

<!-- section: task | Assignment: evaluating a move and making an introduction list -->
The work happens in your Dream 100 spreadsheet from the first lesson of the module. For ten tier A companies, record the city and time zone of their platform teams and budget holders, and count how many are closer to the US coast and how many are closer to your current city. Prepare a list of five companies for an investor or advisor using this lesson's example, and write a forwardable email for one of them. Fill in a budget for the first 90 days in your chosen city using the lesson's categories and real prices. Write people’s time zones in the “time zone” column of the People sheet. The template has no city column and no budget sheet: add them to your own file, and keep the field below for the email.
