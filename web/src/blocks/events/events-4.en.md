<!-- section: hook -->
A CTO submitted “How our platform solves multitenancy problems” to KubeCon and was rejected. His engineer submitted “Isolating customer clusters on shared hardware: three incidents and what we changed,” with a reproducible test and measurements, and was accepted. This lesson covers how program committees select talks, which proposals they reject, how to prepare for a talk, and how to keep it useful after the conference.

<!-- section: theory | How many proposals are accepted -->
Most conferences do not publish acceptance rates. Public materials for PGConf, SREcon, RSAC, NANOG, Datadog DASH, and other events from the event portfolio lesson do not provide this figure; FOSDEM selects talks within individual developer rooms and has no single rate. KubeCon is one of the few conferences that publishes numbers, making it a useful example of the scale involved. CNCF publishes KubeCon submission counts. KubeCon Europe 2024 received 2541 submissions and accepted 223, about 9 %; KubeCon North America 2024 received 1937 and accepted 218, about 11 %; KubeCon Europe 2025 received 2947 submissions, and organizers reported an acceptance rate of about 8 %. KubeCon North America 2025 received 2247 submissions and KubeCon Europe 2026 received 2478, but organizers did not publish a comparable acceptance rate. You cannot divide the number of talks in the program by the number of submissions: the program includes maintainer talks, invited talks, and keynotes that do not go through the general selection process. Final figures for KubeCon North America 2026 had not been published by late November 2026.

These figures suggest that roughly one in ten proposals is accepted into KubeCon's general track, so you cannot rely on a single submission. These figures say nothing about other conferences: their acceptance rates may be higher or lower, so ask the organizer or past speakers how many proposals were received and how many general-track slots were available, and submit several proposals to different events. One person may submit no more than three proposals to KubeCon North America 2026, including those where they are a co-speaker; extra submissions are withdrawn.

<!-- section: theory | What reviewers reject -->
KubeCon's reviewer guidelines require each reviewer to evaluate proposals independently, disclose conflicts of interest, and maintain confidentiality. Generic topics without specifics, product advertising, copied or poor-quality AI-generated proposals, and proposals with little value for the audience receive low scores. A vendor employee can submit a useful technical talk, and analysis of the KubeCon Europe 2024 selection process showed that accepted speakers included vendor employees. However, sponsorship and employer affiliation do not replace value for the audience.

A good abstract answers five questions: what problem the engineer faces, what system and constraints are involved, what the team did, what measurements or failures revealed, and what listeners can reproduce. Here is an example with each answer identified: “A hosting provider wanted customers to create VMs and clusters through the same Kubernetes API as other resources, without access to the shared cluster (problem). We implemented API aggregation on a platform with four hundred customers and a requirement for isolation between them (system and constraints). The talk covers three things: why reconciliation broke on API server restart and how we fixed it; how we restored customer clusters after losing a control-plane node in twelve minutes instead of an hour; and where isolation cost performance and why we accepted that trade-off (work and measurements). Attendees leave with a test reproducing the reconciliation failure on their own cluster (reproducible outcome).” Parentheses identify the questions here; omit them from a real abstract. Include only measurements you actually have: invented deployment scale is noticeable.

<!-- section: theory | Other routes to the stage -->
At many conferences, the general call for proposals is not the only route to the stage. Record these routes separately in the event record: a paid sponsor talk, an invited keynote, a maintainer track, and general selection. At PGConf.EU, sponsor talks run in a separate track from the general call for papers. At vendor user conferences, such as Snowflake Summit or Data + AI Summit, partners get sessions through the organizer's partner programs. At FOSDEM, talks are submitted to a specific developer room, and its charter gives sponsors no preferential treatment for talks.

At KubeCon, foundation projects have separate routes that bypass general selection: maintainer-track talks, ContribFest sessions where attendees contribute to a project together, kiosks in the project area, and project lightning talks, seven-minute introductions before the main conference. KubeCon North America 2025 had 83 project kiosks, 38 project lightning talks, and 71 maintainer-track talks. These formats belong to the project and its maintainers; a company selling a distribution of the project does not acquire that right. CNCF-hosted co-located events, such as ArgoCon and CiliumCon, have their own calls for proposals, and a narrow technical topic often fits them better.

<!-- section: theory | Preparation and the demo -->
PostHog's speaker guide recommends learning about the room and format in advance, building the talk around a real situation, and explaining what broke and what you learned. A demo needs realistic data, readable code and screen content, a tested projector, and a fallback for a network outage; a customer's production account is unsuitable for a demo. Rehearse the entire talk with a colleague acting as an engineer in the audience, including questions and recovery from a failed demo, and remove details that obscure the main result.

<!-- section: theory | Using the talk after the event -->
After the conference, collect the official recording, slides, commands, and any corrections to errors in the talk in one place, and link to it from documentation, the blog, and the newsletter. A talk recording on the conference channel remains useful for years, especially when documentation links to it. One accepted talk can be repeated at meetups and local conferences using the approach from the previous lesson. Count incoming technical questions and repeat lab runs after the talk.

<!-- section: task | Exercise: an abstract for a reviewer -->
Complete the exercise in the “Talk proposal for the program committee” simulator on this page. Write an abstract about a problem your team solved, using the five questions from the lesson. An AI reviewer will assess it against a program committee rubric: audience value, absence of advertising, specificity, novelty, and reproducibility. Rewrite the abstract until every part of the rubric scores at least four out of five, and save the final text.
