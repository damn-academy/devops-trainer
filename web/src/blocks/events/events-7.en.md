<!-- lesson: events.cncf-events | Foundation events for a project: CNCF as a case study | 24 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
In October 2025, KubeVirt held a virtual summit organized by CNCF: 841 people registered and 300 attended. The project was at the Incubating level, and everything the foundation provided for the summit—the platform, registration, call for proposals, and recording on CNCF's YouTube channel—was available to any project at the same level that applied six months ahead. Many projects do not know about this opportunity and spend years organizing such events themselves, on their own platforms and without support. Using CNCF, whose published rules are more detailed than most, this lesson examines what a foundation offers projects for online events and its flagship conference, who is eligible, how to apply, and what the foundation does not promise, then compares this with other foundations and communities.

<!-- section: theory | What depends on project maturity and what depends on company membership -->
CNCF opportunities fall into two categories that are easy to confuse. Some are available to a project based on its maturity level: Sandbox, Incubating, or Graduated. Others are available to a company paying membership dues, based on its membership tier. Company membership does not raise a project's maturity level, and project maturity does not give the company foundation membership benefits. Start by writing down your project's level and whether your company belongs to CNCF, and only then choose where to apply.

A project at any level can hold a foundation-supported virtual summit, give a five-minute project talk at KubeCon, take a kiosk in the Project Pavilion, collaborate with attendees at ContribFest, and speak at local community groups. Incubating and Graduated projects also receive broadcasts on CNCF channels, a dedicated maintainer track at KubeCon, help with major release announcements, and the right to propose an entire event alongside KubeCon. A Gold or Platinum member company can hold four webinars a year, while Silver members get two prerecorded webinars; these webinars are presented by the company under its own name and are unrelated to the project's level.

<!-- section: theory | A virtual project summit -->
For a young project, the foundation's main online opportunity is a virtual summit. Under the 2027 rules CNCF published on 17 September 2026, a project at any level can hold one. The format is standard: a half-day, single-track event with roughly a hundred and fifty minutes of talks, free attendance, and no requirement for sponsors. The program committee must include people from at least three organizations and at least one maintainer; the program must be vendor-neutral, and the foundation makes the final decision on it.

Apply through the form linked in the foundation's guidelines at least six months before your desired date. Describe the event's name and history, format and date, expected attendance, call-for-proposals topics, chairs and program committee members, website, and branding. After approval, CNCF holds a kickoff meeting and monthly calls, sets up the registration platform and Sessionize for talk proposals, and publishes the recording on the foundation's YouTube channel. You and the program committee will do most of the promotion: the foundation does not promise emails and posts across all its channels.

CNCF's 2024 and 2025 reports show attendance figures. The Dapr summit in winter 2024 had 683 registrations and 298 attendees, Strimzi had 660 and 282, EnvoyCon in October 2025 had 441 and 146, and KubeVirt had 841 and 300. Across nine documented events, registrations ranged from 397 to 841 and attendance from 146 to 319, so typically between a third and half of registrants attend. These projects have established communities; for a new Sandbox project, these figures are an upper benchmark, not a promise. The platform also changes: in May 2026, community groups moved from Bevy to Open Community Groups, while the 2027 summit guidelines still mention Bevy, so clarify the platform for your particular event when applying.

<!-- section: theory | Foundation broadcasts, webinars, and publications -->
CNCF's online programs catalog brings together several different formats. Cloud Native Live is a live demo, live coding session, or discussion without slides or a sales pitch; a CNCF ambassador co-hosts it, it streams on YouTube and LinkedIn, and the recording remains on YouTube. Projects qualify at the Incubating or Graduated level. A member company prepares its own prerecorded webinar and submits it through the foundation's marketing form; reruns of KubeCon talks are not accepted. The foundation does not share viewers' contact details in any format.

To understand the scale, look at the catalog's counters: Kyverno's resource optimization broadcast from August 2024 had 528 views, Linkerd's Gateway API broadcast from March 2025 had 765, and Tigera's webinar on live migration with KubeVirt and Calico from August 2026 had 352. These are lifetime recording views; the catalog does not show live viewership. Hundreds of views of a technical demo are useful to a project if the recording is later included in the documentation and linked in answers to questions.

The CNCF blog accepts posts from maintainers of Incubating and Graduated projects, while a member company can write about Sandbox projects within its quota. A response usually takes seven to ten business days, and publication two to three weeks; paid and promotional content is not accepted. The foundation's monthly Wisdom of the Cloud newsletter accepts short news items at the editors' discretion, with no guaranteed placement. For Incubating projects, the foundation helps announce major releases, and when a project moves to a new maturity level it helps with a post and press outreach; for Sandbox admission, it only posts to the technical committee's mailing list. Request these services through the CNCF Service Desk after linking your Linux Foundation account to GitHub.

<!-- section: theory | KubeCon: what a project receives -->
KubeCon offers several separate opportunities for projects, each with its own deadlines. For KubeCon North America 2026, applications for all formats closed on 12 July, and for Project Pavilion kiosks on 22 July, four months before the conference. The maintainer track is a thirty-minute talk for Incubating and Graduated projects; a maintainer on the project's list submits the application. A five-minute project talk is available to any CNCF project, but speakers do not receive a free conference pass. ContribFest provides seventy-five minutes of collaboration with attendees on project tasks; it requires a maintainer with merge rights and a facilitator, and the application explains what people will work on and how it will benefit the project.

Projects at any level can get a Project Pavilion kiosk; Sandbox projects receive one shift. The foundation provides a monitor, cabinet, and sign with the project logo; personal banners and tablecloths are prohibited, and selling a company's product at the kiosk can get a project removed and excluded next time. Kiosks have not included conference passes since 2017. There is also a separate Maintainer Summit the day before KubeCon: maintainers of projects at all levels can attend, but KubeCon registration is required.

Graduated projects can show a sixty-second video before the morning main-stage talks: since 2025, up to ten projects have been featured this way at each KubeCon in Europe and North America. Projects at lower levels do not have this opportunity, and the foundation does not promise a project its own main-stage talk.

<!-- section: theory | An event alongside KubeCon -->
Incubating and Graduated projects can propose an entire event on the day before KubeCon, such as ArgoCon, BackstageCon, or CiliumCon. This is a separate half-day or full-day conference with its own call for proposals, not a talk. CNCF and the Linux Foundation handle the website, submissions, registration, branding, budget and pricing, venue, sound and lighting, security, and coordination with speakers, attendees, and sponsors. The project is expected to provide an active, diverse program committee and at least three potential sponsor companies; maintainers do not have to sell sponsorships, but introduce these companies to the foundation. Deadlines are early: proposals for KubeCon North America 2027 are accepted in March 2027, while applications for KubeCon Europe 2026 closed back on 20 August 2025.

<!-- section: theory | KCD, community groups, and the foundation's technical groups -->
At Kubernetes Community Days, projects at any level apply through the local call for proposals like any other speaker: there is no project quota. If you want to run a KCD in your city, apply through the CNCF form; applications for the second half of 2027 close on 10 January 2027. Most organizers must be local, at least one must be a CNCF ambassador or project maintainer, the program must be neutral, and finances must be transparent.

CNCF community groups, including virtual ones, are the shortest route to your first audience: offer a project talk to an existing group's organizers and agree on its length and recording with them. The foundation's technical groups, or TAGs, discuss project architecture and integrations; after the 2025 restructuring there are five: Developer Experience, Infrastructure, Operational Resilience, Security and Compliance, and Workloads Foundation. People go there for technical feedback and collaboration; the talk will not serve as product advertising.

<!-- section: theory | How other foundations and communities work -->
Other ecosystems structure event opportunities differently, and you should investigate them before planning the year. PostgreSQL has no single organizer: different nonprofits run PGConf.EU, PGConf.dev, and regional PGConf events, each with its own call for papers and sponsor track, while user groups follow the community's sponsor rules. Linux Foundation runs Open Source Summit and community days for its industry foundations, such as OpenSSF, where projects submit talks through general selection. After joining Linux Foundation in 2025, OpenInfra Foundation holds events with other communities. Apache Software Foundation runs its Community Over Code conference and allows communities to hold events using a project's name only under its event policy. If a project belongs to a company and has not been donated to a foundation, partner programs at vendor user conferences, industry conferences, and the company's own events take the foundation's place.

<!-- section: theory | Event naming and neutrality -->
If the company that created a project holds its own event about it, it must consider trademarks. The general rule for all foundations is that, after donation, the project's name belongs to the foundation, and using it in an event name is subject to the foundation's policy. Here is how this works at Linux Foundation, which includes CNCF. After a project is donated to the foundation, its name and logo belong to Linux Foundation, and usage rules distinguish mentioning the project from naming an event. Linux Foundation's policy gives “Infrastructure Day featuring <Project>” as an acceptable name, while “<Project> Day” and “<Project> Day by <Company>” are unacceptable. A project logo may be used in event materials only with written permission, requested from trademarks@linuxfoundation.org. Before announcing your own event, therefore, agree on the name, domain, and designs with the maintainers and foundation lawyers; if you want a neutral event under the project's name, apply through CNCF for a virtual summit or a KubeCon co-located event.

<!-- section: task | Exercise: a year of project events -->
Complete the exercise in the document on this page. If the company has a closed product without an open-source project, build the plan around user conferences run by vendors with partner programs, industry conferences and your own events, and skip the foundation items. Otherwise, use your own project in its foundation or community, or, if you do not have one, the fictional Lumenmesh project: an Incubating Kubernetes networking platform with nine maintainers from Lumenmesh, two from a user bank, and one independent maintainer. Create an event plan for the next twelve months: which foundation or community opportunities are available given the project's level and the company's membership; which you will choose and why; the application deadline for each, counted back from the event date; for CNCF, who will join the virtual summit program committee so that it represents at least three organizations, or, for another foundation or community, which of its conferences and groups you will submit talks to and how submissions work; what the event will be called if you run it yourself, under which policy and with whom you will agree on the name. If a CNCF rule does not apply to your foundation, record that and find your foundation's rule. The plan is ready when each chosen opportunity has an application date, an owner, and the result you want from it.
