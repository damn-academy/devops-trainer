<!-- lesson: events.booth | Working at a booth: three decisive minutes | 20 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
On the first day, two salespeople in matching T-shirts staffed the company's booth and asked every passerby whether they could scan their badge. By evening, they had three hundred scans. On the second day, an engineer from a bank that would be selecting a platform in six months approached the booth and asked about live migration of virtual machines. The answer was “we'll send you materials.” He left for the neighboring booth, where an engineer demonstrated the migration live. This lesson covers what a booth costs, who should staff it, how to conduct conversations, and what to do afterward.

<!-- section: theory | What a KubeCon booth costs -->
The Linux Foundation publishes prices for KubeCon sponsorship packages. For CNCF members in 2026, Diamond cost 235 000 dollars, Platinum 144 000, Gold 84 000, Silver 29 500, and the package for startups, end users, and nonprofits 12 000 dollars; prices for nonmembers are one-fifth higher. The startup package is available to CNCF members less than four years old, with revenue below one million dollars and fewer than fifty employees. Packages include a fixed number of full passes and badge scanners: in 2025, the startup package included one scanner and five passes. Foundation members receive a discount for sponsoring multiple flagship events: 3 % for one, 5 % for two, and 8 % for three or more, if the contracts are signed together.

<!-- section: theory | How many scans and what they mean -->
At KubeCon Europe 2025, booths recorded 97 185 badge scans, an average of 461 per booth; at KubeCon Europe 2026, they recorded 110 213, an average of 557. These are scans: they represent fewer people, meetings, and sales opportunities, and no one publishes how many scans turn into deals. Dash0's founder reported that the company staffed its booth with many engineers and demo stations, dressed everyone in distinctive red overalls, handed out backpacks, ran around 1800 demos, and spent around 250 000 dollars. That is roughly 139 dollars per demo, not a customer acquisition cost.

In his notes on KubeCon Europe 2024, Daniel Bryant described salespeople at some booths aggressively stopping people, with one becoming almost hostile when Daniel declined to hear a presentation. In his view, hard selling does not fit KubeCon's culture. He also writes about young companies that declined to take a booth because of the price or because previous scans had not become deals.

<!-- section: theory | Who staffs the booth and how to talk to visitors -->
A booth needs three roles, and during quiet periods one person can cover two. The first greets people and identifies who has approached. The second is an engineer who answers technical questions and gives demos. The third records agreements and arranges the next step. Allow for breaks and for team members who are giving talks.

A booth conversation lasts around three minutes, during which you need to understand three things: the environment the person works in, the problem they are solving, and their stage in the selection process. Visitors include students, users of the open-source version, engineers running the system in production, and people evaluating the product for purchase. A badge scan does not record these differences, so record them yourself. Show the part of the product relevant to the person's problem and agree on a specific next step.

The demo must work without the exhibition network: keep an environment that can be reset quickly, an offline fallback, and a terminal with a large font. CNCF's branding rules prohibit placing foundation and conference marks on unofficial merchandise and restrict badge lanyard giveaways.

<!-- section: theory | After the conference -->
Assign an owner to each contact and send the promised material within two business days. Count completed meetings and sales-accepted opportunities; scan counts do not constitute results.

<!-- lesson: events.no-booth | Conferences without a booth: meetings, hallway conversations, and side events | 16 -->
<!-- section: hook -->
A founder attended a conference without a booth, paying for his own ticket and arranging twelve meetings a month before the trip. Over three days, he held eleven of those meetings, met two future customers at a dinner for eight infrastructure leaders, and spent less on the trip than the smallest booth would have cost. This lesson covers how to work at a conference without a booth.

<!-- section: theory | Meetings are arranged before the trip -->
The most valuable conference work happens beforehand. Three to six weeks before the event, review published speaker and sponsor lists, find people from target companies, existing users, and partners, and contact them with a specific reason: a question about their talk, an offer to demonstrate a solution to a problem they wrote about, or a dinner invitation. Leave time for important talks and open discussions. DevOpsDays advises sponsors not to rely on badge scanning and to exchange contact details through conversation. Record which meetings took place.

<!-- section: theory | Side events: breakfast, dinner, and roundtables -->
Choose a side-event format based on the conversation you need. A practical breakfast session suits engineers who operate systems. A small dinner suits a group already evaluating the product. A moderated roundtable suits leaders responsible for architecture, risk, and procurement. Agree on the discussion question and guest list in advance, and count accepted invitations, attendees, useful follow-up meetings, and account progress.

Official promotion of a side event through the KubeCon organizer cost confirmed sponsors 10 000 dollars in 2025. That fee covers a listing in the program; the company must pay for and arrange the venue, food, staffing, and guests itself. CNCF rules restrict the use of the conference name in advertising for unofficial events. The 25 000-dollar sponsorship of Maintainer Summit 2026 supports the community and prohibits collecting contacts.

<!-- section: theory | Other conferences -->
Regional conferences have their own terms. DevOpsDays London 2025 offered standard sponsorship with a booth, three tickets, and a one-minute talk for 9000 pounds including VAT, and a logo listing for 1500 pounds. ContainerDays 2025 sold packages ranging from 8000 euros for startups to 35 000 euros. Open Source Summit 2025 offered packages from 10 000 to 90 000 dollars. KCD Czech & Slovak 2025 attracted 265 attendees; KCD Budapest 2025 attracted more than 300. At SCaLE 22x in 2025, Tailscale sponsored the event at the Silver level, spoke to people at its booth, and gave a talk on secure access to home services. The company did not publish scan or deal counts, but noted that more visitors had learned about it at work.

<!-- section: task | Exercise: plan a trip without a booth -->
Complete this exercise in a spreadsheet. Choose an upcoming conference your buyers attend. Find ten people from target companies in the speaker and sponsor lists, and record a reason to contact each one. Plan one side event: format, discussion question, eight guests, venue, and budget. Then calculate the full cost of the trip without a booth and compare it with the cost of the smallest booth, including all the expenses from the course's first lesson.
