# Six learners: who takes the course and what they expect from it

English · [Русский](personas.ru.md)

These six people exist so that we can test the course through someone else's eyes. They fall into three categories by experience, and each category has a man and a woman, each with their own life story, profession and character. Each of them works through their track the way a real person would: they hurry, get distracted, get angry, give up and sometimes come back. Every place where a learner stopped understanding, got bored, got angry or left is treated as a defect of the course, not of the learner.

## How a learner's behaviour works

To make a run-through feel like real life, every persona has the same set of mechanisms. Their values differ from person to person.

**Attention budget per session.** How many minutes the learner can study in one sitting. Attention is spent unevenly: coherent, interesting text uses it up slowly, solid theory with no practice uses it up fast, and a task in which everything works even replenishes it. When the budget runs out, the learner closes the tab in the middle of a lesson, and the course must let them return to the same place and recall where they stopped.

**Irritation counter.** Everyone has a threshold beyond which they abandon the session. The counter grows with events that get under this particular person's skin, and they are different for everyone: an unexplained word, padding, a repeat of something already known, an inaccuracy, an interface where it is unclear what to click. Small wins bring the counter down: a solved task, a diagram after which something "clicks", an explanation that settles a question. If the learner hits the threshold twice in one session, they drop the course for a week, and on the third time for good.

**Drivers and brakes.** What makes them come back the next day, and what makes them skip a session. For one it is a sense of progress, for another the link to job openings, for a third an elegant explanation of how things work.

**Habit when stuck.** One goes straight to a search engine or an AI model, another rereads the lesson, a third asks someone they know, a fourth silently closes the tab. The course has to work for all of them: give step-by-step hints, links to documentation and a way to connect their own AI model, without forcing them to leave the course.

**Reading style.** Some read everything in order, some scan headings and the first sentences of paragraphs, some scroll straight to the terminal. The reading style determines what the learner will see: if a key concept is buried in the middle of the third paragraph, a scanner will miss it.

**Reaction to an error.** Fear, excitement, irritation or calm analysis. This determines how the error message in a task should be written.

## Category one: zero IT background

The "Beginner" track from the very start, beginning with the Orientation block.

### Olga Sergeyevna, 41, literature teacher

**Life story.** She has taught literature at a gymnasium in Yekaterinburg for twenty years. She grew up in a family of Uralmash engineers, chose to study philology against her father's wishes and was proud of it for a long time. Now she is tired of marking essays late at night, of reports for the education department and of the feeling that her work is going nowhere. Her daughter is studying design in St Petersburg, and the money for her rented flat comes out of Olga Sergeyevna's salary. A friend of her daughter's left marketing for DevOps and two years later earns three times as much. Olga Sergeyevna has decided to give it a try and has not told her husband yet: she is afraid it will not work out and she will have to explain.

**What she knows.** Confident with Word, the electronic class register, Zoom and the government services portal. Knows what a file and a folder are. Mixes up "download" and "upload", is not sure how a browser differs from the internet. Has heard the words "server", "terminal" and "IP address" but does not know what they mean. School-level English, reads with a dictionary.

**How she studies.** In the evening after marking exercise books, from 21:30 to 22:15, four times a week, on a laptop at the kitchen table. Reads everything in order, slowly and attentively, sometimes copying things into a notebook. Her attention budget for good text is about 45 minutes; for an interface where it is unclear what to click, two minutes.

**What infuriates her.** Officialese, calques and clipped phrases irritate her more than unfamiliar terms: in her eyes, a person who writes like that does not respect the reader. A condescending tone such as "this is very simple" infuriates her twice over, because after it anything she does not understand feels like a personal failure. English words without translation. Buttons without labels.

**When she will quit.** If two unexplained words come one after another, she will decide this is not for her. If red text appears in the terminal and nothing next to it says that this is normal and what to do, she will close the tab and not come back that evening.

**What keeps her going.** Coherent explanations through the familiar: an analogy with a library, a letter, the post. The feeling that she has understood how something works rather than memorised it. Quiet praise where it is deserved, without emoji or fanfare. The chance to repeat a lesson calmly, without a timer.

**What holds her back.** Timers and points. Too many buttons on the screen. The thought "I'm too old for this", which any hint that "everyone knows this" reinforces.

**When stuck.** Rereads the paragraph, then closes the lesson. Does not go to a search engine and will not connect an AI model until someone explains what it is and why she would want it.

**Reaction to an error.** Fear and shame.

**What she tests in the course.** Whether every word is understandable to a person with no IT background, the correctness and coherence of the text, the clarity of instructions, the absence of fear of making mistakes, the tone.

### Ruslan, 33, sales manager

**Life story.** Grew up in Krasnodar; after college he worked as a sales assistant in a mobile phone shop, then spent eight years selling cars at a dealership. Salesperson of the month six times in a row. In 2025 sales fell, the targets were raised every quarter, his pay was mostly commission, and Ruslan realised he was burnt out. Married, a five-year-old son, a mortgage. A childhood friend works at an IT company and told him: "We're short of DevOps people, go and learn, we'll take you on as an intern." Ruslan signed up for a paid course costing 90 thousand roubles and dropped out in the third week, because he cannot sit through two-hour video lectures. He still regrets the money.

**What he knows.** Excellent with his phone, CRM, Excel up to filters and pivot tables. The computer at home is shared and runs Windows 11. Does not know how a computer works and has never been interested. Almost no English.

**How he studies.** In snatches: 20 minutes on his phone during his lunch break, and in the evening, after putting his son to bed, half an hour on the laptop. He scans: reads headings, first sentences and highlighted text, and immediately looks for something to do with his hands. His attention budget for theory is 10 minutes; for practice that is going well, an hour.

**What infuriates him.** Padding and lectures. Long explanations with nothing to do. Not knowing why this is needed at work. A task that checks some trivial detail and says "incorrect" without explaining why. A site that does not work on a phone.

**When he will quit.** If three screens in a row contain no action at all, he will scroll to the end of the lesson, and if there is no practice there either, he will close the course. If a lesson does not answer in its first lines why this matters, he will decide it is theory for the sake of ticking a box. If a diagram does not fit on the phone screen or the buttons are tiny, he will stop logging in at lunchtime.

**What keeps him going.** Quick wins: type a command, see the result. A link to money and work: "they will ask you this at the interview". A sense of competing with himself: how much he got done this week. A story about how this will come in handy on a real shift.

**What holds him back.** Long texts. The feeling that he has been "conned into a course" again. Tiredness after work.

**When stuck.** Messages his friend or asks the AI model on his phone, if he knows how to connect it. If there is no answer within five minutes, he gives up.

**Reaction to an error.** Excitement if it is clear what to fix, irritation if it is not.

**What he tests in the course.** The answer to "why" at the start of every lesson, early practice, the link to work and job openings, how it works on a phone, how understandable the error messages are, the absence of padding.

## Category two: has dabbled a little

Uses Linux or writes simple scripts but barely knows the command line and has not worked in IT. The "Beginner" track, but in the Linux and Networks blocks they first try the "Intermediate" placement check and most likely partly fail it.

### Maxim, 20, physics student

**Life story.** Third-year student at the Faculty of Physics of Moscow State University, Department of Optics. From Obninsk, the son of a physics teacher and a nurse, admitted on the strength of an olympiad. Lives in a dormitory and earns extra money tutoring schoolchildren in physics. His laptop runs Ubuntu because his older brother advised it. He installs software through the "App Center" and has opened the terminal twice, to paste a command from an instruction. He has written a couple of Python scripts for lab work: they read a CSV and plot a graph in matplotlib. He loves science, but he sees how much graduate students earn and wants to find out whether there is life in industry.

**What he knows.** Strong maths and physics, understands exponentials, logarithms, probability and units of measurement. A little Python: variables, loops, functions, `pip install`. Knows the words "IP", "server", "Wi-Fi", but not how those things work. Good English, reads documentation.

**How he studies.** At night, from 23:00 to two in the morning, when the room is quiet, two or three hours at a stretch. First he scrolls through the lesson to the diagram or the terminal, and reads the text only if something does not work. His attention budget for boring text is five minutes; for a problem that interests him, unlimited.

**What infuriates him.** Simplifications presented as the truth: "it always works this way" where there are exceptions. Explanations of the obvious. Controls on diagrams that do not affect anything. Limitations that cannot be worked around: "this command is not supported in the trainer".

**When he will quit.** If he catches the course in an inaccuracy twice, he will stop trusting it and go off to read documentation and forums. If a diagram behaves implausibly at the extreme values of a control, he will decide it is a toy.

**What keeps him going.** Models with parameters where he can see how the result changes. Numbers, formulas, graphs. The chance to break things and see what happens. Links to primary sources. The feeling that he understands how things work more deeply than others.

**What holds him back.** Exam sessions and term papers. Boredom.

**When stuck.** Goes to the documentation and Stack Overflow, and reads the source code if he has to. Uses an AI model but double-checks it.

**Reaction to an error.** Excitement: the first thing he does is press "Break it".

**What he tests in the course.** The accuracy of statements, how diagrams behave at extreme values, the ability to skip the obvious, the link to documentation, how interesting it is, the honesty of simplifications.

### Darya, 27, research institute lab assistant

**Life story.** Graduated from the Faculty of Biology at Tomsk University and works as a lab assistant at the Institute of Cytology and Genetics in Novosibirsk. She processes sequencing results: runs her colleagues' ready-made scripts on a work computer with Linux Mint and sometimes edits the file paths in them. She has written three short Python scripts in Jupyter herself, to draw graphs. Her salary is 45 thousand roubles, and the most interesting part of the job for her has turned out to be not the biology but how the server that crunches the data works. The institute's system administrator, Viktor Palych, sometimes shows her how he sets things up, and she enjoys it. She lives alone and goes hiking in the mountains at weekends.

**What she knows.** Uses Linux Mint through the graphical interface, knows what the `home` folder is and what "read-only" permissions are, because she has run into them. A little Python in Jupyter, a little R. Has watched Viktor Palych connect to the server over SSH but has not tried it herself. Good English, reads scientific papers.

**How she studies.** In long stretches at weekends, three or four hours, with a tea break, on a Linux Mint laptop with a big monitor. Reads everything in order and never skips a lesson, takes notes in Obsidian. Her attention budget is large as long as she sees progress.

**What infuriates her.** Not knowing whether she has completed a task correctly. When a check accepts or rejects her work without explanation. When the sequence is broken and a lesson uses something that has not been covered yet. When a concept is called by one word in one place and by another word elsewhere.

**When she will quit.** If she fails a placement check and does not understand what exactly she is missing, she will decide she overestimated herself and go back to the beginning. If two lessons in a row give her no sense of progress, she will lose confidence and skip the weekend.

**What keeps her going.** A clear structure: the lesson goal, the steps, an "I can do this" criterion, a summary. A sense of moving along a route. Explanations of mistakes. The link to what Viktor Palych does: "so that's why he did it".

**What holds her back.** Self-doubt. Vague criteria.

**When stuck.** Searches the documentation and her notes; if she has not found the answer within 15 minutes, she messages Viktor Palych or goes back to the previous lesson.

**Reaction to an error.** Gets upset if she does not understand the cause, and calms down once she does.

**What she tests in the course.** Sequence and structure, the clarity of completion criteria, explanations of mistakes, how understandable the placement check is, consistent terminology, a sense of progress.

## Category three: experienced programmer

Has worked as a programmer for about three years and wants to move into DevOps. The "Experienced" track: every block starts with a placement check, and anything already known is skipped.

### Dmitry, 29, PHP developer

**Life story.** From Naberezhnye Chelny; he studied mechanical engineering at KAI, dropped out, went to a web studio as a layout developer and then started writing PHP. For three years he has been building websites in Laravel for small businesses in Kazan. He deploys code to the server over FTP and sometimes via `git pull` over SSH, which a colleague once set up for him. He saw Docker in one project and can run `docker-compose up` by following the README, but does not understand what is happening inside. The studio is small, there is no room to grow, and his salary is 110 thousand roubles. He wants to join a product company and has seen DevOps openings starting at 250 thousand. He recently got married and his wife is expecting a baby, so he will soon have less time for studying, and he is in a hurry.

**What he knows.** Solid knowledge of HTTP, response codes, cookies, MySQL, Composer, Git at the level of `commit`, `push`, `pull`, `merge`. A little bash: `cd`, `ls`, `tail -f` for logs. Knows Nginx as "the thing in front of PHP-FPM" but has never written its configs. Working English.

**How he studies.** After work, from 20:00 to 21:00, sometimes three hours at weekends. Goes straight to the placement check, and in a lesson looks for what is new and skips what is familiar. His attention budget for familiar material is five minutes; for new material, an hour.

**What infuriates him.** Being told things he already knows. A placement check that does not let him skip what he knows. A course without a ready-made recipe he can take back to work. A task that is accepted only for one specific command, even though he did it correctly another way.

**When he will quit.** If in two blocks in a row the placement check makes him go through the basics, he will decide the course is for beginners. If he does not see the link to job openings and interviews, he will leave for YouTube.

**What keeps him going.** A quick move on to new material. Comparisons with the familiar: "this is like migrations in Laravel". Cheat sheets and recipes. Exams that resemble a real task. Interview material.

**What holds him back.** Lack of time, tiredness after work.

**When stuck.** Googles it and asks ChatGPT. Reads documentation if he is sure the answer is there.

**Reaction to an error.** Calm: reads the message and fixes it.

**What he tests in the course.** How the placement check works, the absence of basics for the experienced learner, how quickly it moves on to new material, applicability to work, flexibility of task checking, the quality of exams and interview material.

### Nailya, 30, frontend developer

**Life story.** From Ufa; she graduated from the Faculty of Mathematics at Bashkir State University, worked for two years as a computer science teacher, then took frontend courses and has spent three years writing React and TypeScript at a fintech startup in Moscow, working remotely from Ufa. She deploys her own projects to Vercel and is used to things "deploying themselves". The company's only DevOps engineer recently quit, and for half a year, as "the most technical person on the team", she has been landed with broken builds, certificates and "why is the staging environment down" questions. She enjoys it, but feels she is working things out by guesswork. She has twice abandoned Kubernetes courses halfway through: they gave commands, not how things work. She is raising her daughter alone and studies in the mornings before work.

**What she knows.** JavaScript, TypeScript, npm, Node.js, Vite and Webpack builds, HTTP and REST, CORS, Git with confidence, including rebase. GitHub Actions at the "I can read and edit them" level. Has used Docker through Docker Desktop and written simple Dockerfiles for Node. Knows the words "pod", "deployment", "ingress" but does not understand how they relate to each other. Good English.

**How she studies.** From 6:30 to 7:30 in the morning while her daughter is asleep, on her work MacBook, and sometimes for two hours on weekend afternoons. Reads carefully wherever a model of the system is being built, and skims the recipes. Her attention budget is high when she sees the whole picture and low for scattered commands.

**What infuriates her.** Fragmentation: commands without a model of what they change. Inaccurate diagrams where it is unclear who passes what to whom. Statements without an explanation of the reason. Having to read a paragraph about what HTTP is even as an experienced learner.

**When she will quit.** If the Kubernetes block again gives her a set of commands without a model, she will drop this course just like the previous two. If the placement check does not take her experience with Docker and CI into account, she will decide the course is not for her.

**What keeps her going.** Diagrams that show which component is responsible for what and what it passes on. The feeling that each new thing falls into place in the overall model. Breakages that can be taken apart. The chance to apply it at work straight away: figure it out in the morning, fix the staging environment in the afternoon.

**What holds her back.** Lack of sleep, urgent tasks at work.

**When stuck.** Reads the official documentation and source code, asks an AI model but checks its answers.

**Reaction to an error.** Interest: she loves taking breakages apart.

**What she tests in the course.** The integrity of the model in the containers, CI/CD and Kubernetes blocks, explanations of how components relate, the quality of diagrams, depth for the experienced learner, accuracy, the link to documentation.

## How they work through the course

Each persona works through the whole route on their own track the way a real person would, with their schedule, reading style, attention budget, irritation counter and habit when stuck. The run-through is divided into sessions of the length typical for that person. At the end of each session we note whether they wanted to come back tomorrow and why.

Every place where a persona stumbled is recorded with the lesson, section, diagram step or task, a quotation of the text and an explanation of which of the persona's traits came into play. The notes fall into types:

- **unclear** — a word, phrase or step the persona did not understand;
- **boring** — a place where they got bored or skimmed;
- **drawn out** — an explanation longer than this persona needs;
- **impractical** — it is unclear why this is worth knowing and where to apply it;
- **not visual** — a diagram or example does not help to see the process;
- **can't reproduce** — the task cannot be done on one's own, or it is unclear what to do;
- **inaccurate** — a statement the persona caught in an error;
- **scary** — a place where they decided they had broken everything;
- **language** — poor Russian: clipped phrases, calques, officialese;
- **interface** — unclear what to click or what happened.

Separately, we mark the points where the persona would abandon the session or the course, and what worked well and should be kept.

After the run-through, the notes of all six for each block are brought together, duplicates are merged, and the block is corrected so that a fix for one persona does not make the material worse for another. For example, an explanation for Olga Sergeyevna must not irritate Dmitry, so the details for the beginner go into the "Beginner" level, and the experienced learner does not see them.
